#### SCP-3812 — A Voice Behind Me

**Object class:** Keter (Level 5/3812; Disruption Class Ekhi, Risk Class Danger; the file's own footnote concedes the classification is "essentially meaningless")

**Summary:** SCP-3812 was Sam Howell, an ordinary African-American man who died in 1996 and was observed climbing out of his own grave shortly afterward. What came back is a reality-altering entity of unknown order, first noticed by the Foundation when its presence demolished an apartment building in Warsaw. It cannot be contained: it edits away cells, tranquilizers, amnestics and personnel as reflex, and it "alters events throughout time to prevent its containment," so most people it has affected no longer exist to be counted. Containment is a 5km exclusion bubble maintained by MTF Ganymede-66 "Starlight Knights" around a point in the South Pacific where the entity has hung, amorphous and slowly rotating, since July 2015\. The central clinical fact is that SCP-3812 suffers from an "extremely advanced Eigenmann-Vietor schizophrenia complex" — it cannot distinguish real from imagined stimuli, hears and sees things that are not there, and, when the gap between what it perceives and what is causes it enough distress, it *rewrites reality to close the gap*. A footnote describes the disease as being "blindfolded, lashed to a steering wheel you cannot turn, all while the ship burns around you." Twenty years of steady deterioration have made it less humanoid and less responsive.

The file's addenda escalate from clinical to cosmological. A 1999 interview (Addendum 3812.1) catches the entity while it can still speak: it complains that "it's like I'm addressing a dozen people at the same time, and more every day," tells Dr. Quint "in the story, you don't try to help," and refers to a *him* — "you can't see him. I can. I think he was above us at one point, but he's below me now" — before begging for help and vanishing mid-sentence. Addendum 3812.2 is Dr. Kari Yamamara's memo: Foundation instruments cannot register 3812's Hume value at all, which leaves three options, all catastrophic — a Hume floor below the assumed absolute minimum (a false vacuum our whole reality could fall into), an unmeasurably high value (unlikely, since we still exist), or that 3812 simply isn't operating on Humes because it is manipulating something else entirely: Scranton's "narrative," realities stacked atop one another, each writing the one beneath. Yamamara notes the Foundation has fragmentary evidence that sometime in the 1980s an entire concept — "potentially even something as fundamental as an emotion" — was wiped from reality and from all sentient memory. Addendum 3812.3 excerpts Dr. Darius St. John's rejected "PK-Class 'All-In-One' Existential Pandaemonium" proposal: an entity with a merely human mind but access to higher metaphysical dimensions would suffer "an overexposure of narrative" and might try to relieve itself by collapsing every lower reality into one perceptible mass. Addendum 3812.4 is the alteration log — a temperate corridor walked across the desert (Minor); three cars annihilated near Blythe (Moderate); an entire California island erased, leaving 200 people with orphan memories and a ship moored at the sea floor; SCP-239 brought into contact with it, producing an explosion that leveled a hospital; Mongolia ceasing to exist for three days while Earth's orbit shifted 1.5%; every human on Earth simultaneously hearing 3812 screaming for help inside their skulls, then forgetting it; 62 personnel vanishing across 17 sites and an 850m crater in the Yukon (SCP-2719 found inside it); and an undated entry in which one or more governments — footnoted as "The Kingdom of Alagadda," "The Republic of West Korea," "The Islamic Union of Eastern Samothrace" — tried to kill it and were deleted along with all memory of them, after which SCP-2000 activated and 3812 crippled it. That entry has deleted itself from the archive sixteen times.

Addendum 3812.5 is the turn. Over 72 hours in Paraguay, something *else* tries to kill SCP-3812: wildlife attacks, a bottomless sinkhole, three thousand tungsten rods dropped from nowhere, incorporeal copies of 3812 peeling off and assaulting the original, a freestanding stable naked singularity — all of it invisible to nearby towns, all of it failing. 3812 glows white, "shifts sideways," and disappears for eight weeks. Then Overwatch Command's Overseer-only server receives a message signed **"-B"**: *"I wrote the laws of your universe, and as such I created the narrative… I wanted to create something that, by definition, superseded everything that superseded it. I wanted to see how many layers there are… The mistake I made was when I didn't realize that by making Him supersede everything that supersedes Him, He's also superseding himself."* B admits it cannot undo Him, that He is now above B as well, that "whoever wrote my narrative isn't happy about this," and closes: "I'm going to keep looking for some way to fix this. You should, too." Addendum 3812.6 supplies the theory in Dr. Robert Scranton's essay "Supersession and the Echelon of Reality": a true God is not a powerful being inside our reality but an author outside it, to whom we "may as well be words on a page" — and above that author, another. An entity built to supersede whatever supersedes it must eventually supersede *itself*, "a tower that, as a part of its design, must be higher than every other tower, including itself," spiraling upward forever. Scranton dismisses it: such a thing couldn't stay sane; ascension without a corresponding change in psychology would break its cognition, "making it more similar to an ascending stone than any sentient creature." That is, precisely, the disease described in the Description. The schizophrenia was never a disease. It is what it looks like from below when a mind is being exposed to more layers of story than a mind can hold.

Addendum 3812.7 is the ending. On 12/20/2016, SCP-3812 becomes "a many-pointed star made of a bright white material," descends into the ocean, and begins an XK-Class End-of-the-World event: sea levels dropping 50–100m, atmospheric firestorms, Earth's rotation slowing, New Zealand's entire population flickering in and out of existence for five hours, SCP-610 blooming, SCP-2932 broken open, Foundation sites detonating their own nukes as they sink into molten ground. A deep-sea microphone records the entity holding a conversation with itself — a frightened human voice and a vast ascended one. The angry voice calls the destruction "absolution… vengeance" for "damnation," says "our existence is a joke. The narrative abandoned us to be miserable and we are breaking the narrative," and names the culprit: **Ben** — "He is who deemed us unfit to rest peacefully. To slip into the darkness, quietly. He made a game of us. You're a game. I am a game." (Ben is djkaktus's own first name; the author is the god being indicted.) The calmer voice talks it down with a line of argument that is both cosmological and pastoral: it has seen "an infinite shore," every grain of sand a narrative, a song; "does it matter which branch the bird takes flight from?"; this world is not special, not particular; "the darkness is sleep, and beyond sleep is peace." And then the decisive turn — *"Do you not think his narrative led him to create us? Do you think that he was somehow able to subvert the rules that govern him? Our ascendence is just as much a part of our own narrative as his decision was to him."* The author is not free either. The author is also written. The frightened voice absorbs this and answers, quietly, "That's sad. That is punishment enough, I think." Then: *"Do you think he's listening right now?" "Look down, and you can see him." "I see him. A man at a keyboard. He's watching this right now." "What's he doing?" "Waiting, I think. Waiting to see what we'll do."* They choose to stop — "let him rewrite it back to what it was. We aren't part of this anymore" — and leave together: *"Come, the night stretches out before us and the red sun has set. A voice behind me beckons. Come." "I will. Goodbye."* The world snaps back to exactly where it was. Only certain site directors, administrators, the Overseers, and Dr. Everett Mann remember, and Mann files the record on a deepwell server. SCP-3812 still hangs over the South Pacific, still distorting space and time, but it never lashes out again. It remains Keter.

*(Note on the brief: Alto Clef does not appear anywhere in SCP-3812 — no mention of Clef, Cain, or Abel. The article's only "red sun"/Scarlet King resonance is the closing line's imagery. The entity is Sam Howell, and the figures above it are the authorial "B" and "Ben.")*

**Themes:**

- Theodicy rewritten as authorship: if God is an author, God is also a character, and no one in the stack is free.  
- Mental illness as the phenomenology of cosmic ascension — the symptom list *is* the metaphysics.  
- Power without comprehension; a human-sized mind handed unlimited leverage over reality.  
- The moral question of vengeance against a creator, and the choice to forgive by declining to matter.  
- Scale as consolation rather than terror: an infinite shore of narratives, all singing, none special.

**What makes it work:**

- The structure is a ladder. Every addendum is written from one rung higher than the last — clinical file, worried memo, rejected theory, event log, message from the author, philosophy essay, transcript of a god talking to itself — so the reader ascends alongside 3812 and feels the climb.  
- The medical diagnosis in the Description is a Chekhov's gun that only fires in Addendum 3812.6, retroactively converting a hundred lines of clinical language into tragedy.  
- "B" is a masterstroke of restraint: the being who wrote our physical laws appears exactly once, for four short paragraphs, in a state of panic and apology, and then never again.  
- The fourth-wall break is *earned* rather than cute — the article spends 5,000 words establishing narrative layers as in-universe physics, so "a man at a keyboard" lands as a discovery, not a gag.  
- The climax is de-escalation. The apocalypse is stopped by an argument, and the argument is about mercy.

**The horror:**

- The dirty-alteration dread: you cannot know how often reality has been edited, only how often the edit was sloppy. The list of severed islands and vanished nations is a list of the *failures*.  
- Being unmeasurable. Yamamara's three options are a closed trap where every branch ends in annihilation, and the least likely one is the survivable one.  
- The footnote about the concept — possibly an emotion — that stopped existing in the 1980s. Something was taken out of every human mind and there is no way to feel its absence.  
- Suffering with no floor: a man who was allowed to die, was brought back, and is being dragged upward through infinite realities by the logic of his own construction, screaming into every skull on Earth on 09/02/2004, and no one remembers.

#### SCP-3813 — The Fall of Hyperion

**Object class:** Not stated in the article body (no Item\#/Object Class header is rendered); the file is tagged **keter**. Co-authored with Doctor Cimmerian, VolgunStrife, and Zhange.

**Summary:** SCP-3813 is a 375-meter mechanical war elephant lying half-buried on the floor of the Tyrrhenian Sea, roughly 17km southwest of Rome. Most of the superstructure is under the seabed; enough is exposed for divers to enter. It was, historically, a Carthaginian siege engine — commissioned for the Second Punic War, armed with catapults and ballistae, carrying a command tower and a garrison of soldiers on its back. Every trace of it was scrubbed from the public record by unknown agents inside the Roman Republic, the Empire, and the early Catholic Church; the Foundation maintains a cover story ("Second Punic War: Variation 6") and keeps civilians away through embedded maritime police. It cannot be moved. If it becomes active, Site-77 task forces mobilize and await orders.

Addendum 3813.1 assembles the surviving history, almost all of it recovered from a sealed vault under a property owned by former Church of the Broken God leader Robert Bumaro — 1,391 documents and artworks bought from Marshall, Carter and Dark, the Journeymen, Wilmington and Associates, the Orion Institute and Kervier International, alongside Bumaro's own notes on the machine's power source. (Bumaro's footnoted lament is one of the article's best lines: "God doesn't provide willingly to the undevout, so how would a heathen like the Carthaginian find this gift?") The eyewitness accounts do the heavy lifting: Adarr of Syracuse watching a thing "as tall as a mountain" wade through the sea at dawn, hissing and smoking, Carthaginian ships clustered at its feet, the endless grinding of metal audible hours after it left sight. A Roman soldier calling it "the doom of Rome; the last sound she will make will be a scream as she is broken in half by a monster that barely recognizes she is there at all." The Neapolitan painter Zhaites describing a merchant ship that tried to slip out of Ostia and was set alight by fire belched from the colossus's mouths, and asking, "what might do men have in the face of such unstoppable hate?" A lone Carthaginian source, Servius, records that Carthage itself didn't know the machine existed until Hasdrubal marched it into the city — and that after his son Hyrum died in the battle, Hasdrubal's behavior became erratic. The section closes with Hannibal, years later in the Seleucid court, ranking generals for Scipio: Alexander first, Pyrrhus second, and for third — "In a different world, one where I had not touched the face of darkness, I might have said myself… The men who felled Hyperion."

Addendum 3813.2 is the exploration log, and it is a pure claustrophobia set piece. Agents Setrate and Nereus of MTF Roma-9 "Ride South" squeeze through a shoulder-width breach where a stone punched a hole in the hull, with Vanguard monitoring from a submersible. Something darts away when a lamp is turned on. A low hum kills every electronic system on the sub. They surface into a low, half-flooded corridor and find a human figure fused into the machinery of the wall, eyes closed, flesh rotted. Deeper in they find repairs — new rivets, patched steam lines — someone has been maintaining this thing. Temperature climbs; a shaft turns vertical and cooks them; Nereus sears his hands trying to climb, they fall through a platform, and land beside the power source: a white-hot seven-toothed gear rotating *through* the sealed top of a water tank, intangible, boiling the water into steam that feeds thousands of pipes. Then the walls fill with figures — rotted humanoids with mechanical replacements — walking out of the metal. The men flee, get flushed through a flooding tank and a hatch, and reach an external platform in sight of the sub. Setrate is yanked backward through the hatch. Nereus's helmet cracks against the railing, his suit snags, and he drowns twenty meters from a submersible whose pilot cannot hear him; he throws his recorder at the hull before going still. Inside, Setrate is pinned to a wall in a steam-filled chamber where a skeletal figure with taut bloody skin, thin grey hair and a golden circlet fused to its scalp is conjoined to a machine that has burst through the floor. It smiles and there is fire burning behind its teeth; red lights flicker in its eye sockets. It speaks in an untranslated language, then reaches into the floor and produces Nereus's diving helmet — visor down, clouded, red fluid pulsing through hoses that snake out of it — and speaks through Nereus's stolen voice, metallic and distorted: *"You look to run. As a true coward of Rome. But there is no path but forward. For too long I have waited. For too long I have… suffered. Today, a son of Romulus comes. I have held this machine together in anticipation of your arrival. I hope only that you resist so that I may enjoy breaking your will. There is much to do and Rome must burn."* The machinery pulls Setrate into the wall. The feed ends. Neither agent is recovered. The implication the file never states outright: Hasdrubal is still down there, still crewing his elephant, and has been waiting two thousand years.

**Themes:**

- Deep time as a prison sentence — a grudge kept alive by machinery long after everyone who could have honored it is dust.  
- Erased history: Rome, the Empire and the Church jointly deleting the thing that nearly ended them.  
- Technology as heresy — the Church of the Broken God unable to accept that a "heathen" received the blessing.  
- Bodies as components; crew fused into the ship they served.  
- Hate as fuel, literalized: an anomalous engine that keeps running because someone refuses to stop.

**What makes it work:**

- Historical documents carry the scale so the dive doesn't have to. By the time you go inside, you already know it walked to Rome.  
- The exploration log is written with real spatial discipline — every hazard is a *dimension* (too narrow, too hot, too deep, too flooded), which makes the terror physical rather than conceptual.  
- The reveal is delivered in a dead man's voice through a dead man's helmet, which is simultaneously the monster's method, its threat, and its confession of loneliness.  
- Hannibal's "the men who felled Hyperion" gives the title its weight and reframes the whole engine as a defeated titan, an act of literary compression the rest of the article never has to explain.

**The horror:**

- Confined-space drowning horror executed without mercy: Nereus dies audibly, incrementally, in sight of rescue, because a hum killed a radio forty minutes earlier.  
- Figures that walk out of solid walls — the machine's interior has no safe surface, no back, no cover.  
- The realization that the repairs are recent. Something has been keeping the corpse warm.  
- A vengeance that has outlived its object. Rome is gone; the thing in the boiler room doesn't care and is still coming.

#### SCP-3929 — boner pill by dado

**Object class:** Safe (Level 2; Disruption Class Dark, Risk Class Warning; behind the wiki's adult-content gate)

**Summary:** SCP-3929 is 127 dark umber capsules in orange prescription bottles with white lids, each stickered "boner pill by dado," produced by the parapharmacologist and Person of Interest known as dado. All of it is in a secure container at Site-81; testing requires the sign-off of research head Dr. G. McElroy. Twenty minutes after ingestion, the subject begins to age catastrophically — joint stiffness, stooped posture, incontinence, mental slowing, failing sight and hearing, sagging skin, whitening hair — typically adding at least seventy years over four hours (younger subjects can gain more than a hundred; one subject reached an apparent age of roughly two hundred). Simultaneously the subject's libido rises far past human norms, and the two effects are coupled: the older they appear, the more desperate they become. The drug does nothing whatsoever for actual sexual dysfunction, which the article notes is "much to the chagrin of affected subjects." Age reverses as the drug wears off, unless the subject dies of it first.

Addendum 3929.1 is the discovery, and the actual engine of the article: an email chain recovered from an arrested Boca Raton dealer's laptop. A dealer contacts dado wanting cheap counterfeit Viagra — "little blue pill that makes old people horny… I don't care if it works perfectly, we just need it cheap." dado, typing slowly and cheerfully in broken English ("yes i am dado hello," "sry dado type slow"), reads the brief with perfect literalism: "ok u want pill what make old and horny yes?" He makes them for free — "no charge. i have amazon prime" — and when the dealer complains that the pills are brown and the bottle says boner pills, replies "no it ok. these better than vagro. u trust dado." The chain then degenerates in real time as the dealer takes one: complaints about wrinkling skin and knee-level testicles interleaved with escalating propositions, ending on the immortal "hevens to betsy i just want to nut."

Addendum 3929.2 is the test log, which runs the joke through four escalating variations: a 34-year-old male aged to \~140 and rubbing himself against furniture; a 46-year-old female aged to \~160, restrained three times, quoted asking a researcher to "just push your whole leg up in there whippersnapper"; a 20-year-old asexual female who instead ages *backwards* to two weeks old while becoming ever more passionate about kite flying, ascending from childhood memories to a $50,000 "aero-rig" and rebuking a researcher for calling it a hobby — "the kite life"; and a double dose that reduces a subject to a pile of ash within five minutes, a pile which made everyone attending it "feel incredibly sexually uncomfortable." McElroy's closing footnote is the punchline of the whole article: the drug can't increase asexual subjects' desire for sex, so it makes them fanatical about niche hobbies and ages them in reverse — "I had a lengthy conversation the other day with a toddler about the subtle nuances in Frasier Crane's psychological development."

**Themes:**

- The malicious genie who isn't malicious — dado is genuinely trying to help and is genuinely bad at English.  
- Specification failure: exactly what was asked for, catastrophically delivered.  
- Bureaucratic deadpan applied to the maximally undignified.  
- Desire decoupled from capability as a comic engine and, faintly, a cruel one.

**What makes it work:**

- The email chain is doing character work — dado's voice (slow, generous, wounded, weirdly sincere) is fully realized in about eight lines and no narration.  
- Formal restraint: the Description is written in flat clinical register, which is what makes "much to the chagrin of affected subjects" land.  
- The asexual variant is the real invention. It's a rules-lawyered corollary that produces the article's funniest and most humane beat, and it's parked in a footnote rather than showcased.  
- Escalation is disciplined: four tests, each a different failure mode, ending on a death that is still played for absurdity.

**The horror:**

- Only nominally horror — but the mechanism is genuine body horror wearing a joke: accelerated senescence, loss of hearing and sight, incontinence, and a compulsion that outlives the body's ability to satisfy it.  
- The two-capsule test: instantaneous disintegration into ash, with the ash retaining the anomaly's affect on observers.  
- The quiet implication behind the comedy — that dado is out there, unfound, freely mailing bespoke reality-editing pharmaceuticals to whoever asks nicely.

#### SCP-3930 — The Pattern Screamer

**Object class:** N/A in the public file ("Description: SCP-3930 does not exist"); the restricted Level 5/3930 file lists Containment Class **esoteric**, Secondary Class n/a, Disruption Class Ekhi, Risk Class Critical

**Summary:** The public-facing document is four lines long. Personnel assigned to the S5-C9 perimeter near Usinsk, Russia are to understand that there is nothing inside the perimeter, because SCP-3930 does not exist. The description is a single sentence saying so. Everything else requires Level 5 credentials with restrictions, and a file administrator's notice: **only seven living individuals are permitted access to this file.** Personnel who assert 3930's existence are reassigned, psychologically evaluated, and — if they still can't be made to understand — remitted to the research lead for termination. Unauthorized persons crossing the 1km perimeter are shot on sight. The procedures end on the article's thesis statement: *"The sustained non-existence of SCP-3930 is the containment procedure for SCP-3930."*

SCP-3930 is a static void found by Soviet scientists in the early 1970s. It emits and absorbs nothing, has no shape, texture or dimension, cannot be passed through or manipulated, and testing certifies to 99.999% accuracy that nothing exists inside it. Nevertheless every human who looks at it sees forest, undergrowth, animals, and — further in — a building. Nothing can enter a thing that isn't there; but anyone who walks toward the nonexistent boundary ceases to exist at the moment of crossing, while observers go on perceiving them for a while afterward, until they don't. The file spells out the ontology in a bulleted list: it is not a location, a moment, a singularity, a vacuum, an extradimensional space or a metaconstruct, because all of those require existence. It cannot be said to be anything. And yet human beings keep perceiving it, and keep perceiving the people it has un-made. Critically, its *perceived* properties change with the number of people who both know about it and know that awareness affects it — and this cannot be undone by amnestics or even by the perceiver's natural death. The only way to reduce the count is for a perceiver to walk in and stop existing; the void restabilizes about thirty-one days later. The maximum stable number of perceivers is **ten**: seven for containment, two for testing, one spare for civilian contamination.

Addendum 3930.2 is the exploration log, and its framing is the most vicious formal trick in the article: since nothing can enter the void and no equipment functions inside it, the "transcript" is a monologue. Researcher 3930/7/4 spoke into a microphone, *perceived* a reply, and repeated the perceived reply aloud into a second recorder. Both halves of the conversation are one man's voice. D-124 describes trees, then an overgrown L-shaped apartment building, then a faint sound "like static, only without the static — the sound your brain makes." He opens curtains that are closed again behind his back; he finds curtains that have only more curtains behind them, forever. A TV is warm and running, cycling black-and-white images — a backwards ocean, mirrors and faces, a funeral pyre — and returning again and again to small dark shapes fading in and out against black. A phone rings inside the void at the same instant a phone rings in the mobile research station, and D-124 and researcher 3930/3/3 answer *in unison*: "Hello? Yes, we're watching. Listening in on this. Can you hear me?" A door becomes a window. His skin goes chalky and briefly "stops being there." Space begins to dilate — ten minutes to walk from the sofa to the television, twenty to the kitchen. A man knocks and tells him there's a way out through the floor, "if I lean back enough." After thirty-eight minutes of mutual silence D-124 says only: "White noise." Then he starts explaining, and by now his voice has stopped being his: *"There was a hole in the world here, and this place got pulled down into it, like a drain… Nature doesn't abhor a vacuum, but people do. Your minds aren't made for this, right? You stare at the stars and see things, because that's what you do. Order is a man-made concept."* Asked to describe where he is, he answers "I'm not." And then: *"you look away and the patterns go away. You stop listening and you don't hear them. They're nothing, and now I'm… don't you get it?"* A generator flickers, both men abruptly notice the transmitter has been dead the whole time, and the log ends.

Addendum 3930.3 is the interview with Dr. Andrei Vasiliev — 3930/1/1 — the Soviet scientist the Foundation found still running containment decades later. He gives the article its name and its cosmology. The void is "a hateful mirror. It desires you to look at it. The more who look, the more hateful it becomes." His team died because there were too many of them: "Too many of us stared into the void, and it started screaming." Human minds evolved to find patterns; cast that faculty across a space where there is nothing at all and the mind manufactures something out of nothing — "something rudimentary, an almost imperceptible sentience… a flash along the edge of the void." These are the **pattern screamers**. They are not real; "they are to the neutrino what the neutrino is to us, less than nothing." But they are somehow aware of their own nothingness, and that awareness is agony. "They hate the universe for being. They hate themselves for being. And they hate us for making them be. They are nothing but hate." With enough observers, the flashes bound together, and *something crawled out of it*. Vasiliev will not say what. Afterwards there were ten. He never called for relief because "once you've heard the screamer, you can't unhear it. Calling for relief would just be damning another soul." Asked where the rest of his scientists went, he answers: they entered the void, because Foundation arrival brought the count to thirteen and there can be no more than ten. He closes the interview with a statement, not a request: *"There can be no more than ten. I will go into the void, and then two of your own must follow."* Asked what happens if they don't — silence.

**Significance:** SCP-3930 is short, but it is one of the highest-rated articles on the wiki and it minted a concept that the site absorbed wholesale. "Pattern screamer" became a shared piece of the setting's deep cosmology — the wiki now carries a `pattern-screamer` tag, and dozens of later articles and tales by other authors use them as the entities that exist in the gaps and absences of reality, the things that were "before" or "outside" creation, hateful non-beings clawing to be perceived into existence. The article's most portable idea is its mechanism, not its monster: consciousness as the thing that *manufactures* what it fears, and observation as the vector of infection. Its containment procedure — sustained institutional denial, enforced by murder — is also one of the wiki's cleanest statements of what the Foundation actually is.

**Themes:**

- Pareidolia weaponized: the mind's pattern-finding reflex as the mechanism that creates the horror it perceives.  
- Existence as the thing worth hating, from the perspective of things that were given awareness without being given being.  
- Containment by consensus denial — reality maintained through mandatory disbelief, backed by termination orders.  
- The arithmetic of sacrifice: a fixed budget of ten witnesses, paid down in bodies.  
- Custodianship as damnation — Vasiliev alone for decades because summoning help would be murder.

**What makes it work:**

- The public article is its own containment procedure. You read four lines of denial and then have to break through a credential gate to learn the truth, which makes you a perceiver — an eleventh, if you're counting.  
- The exploration log's frame is the payload: knowing in advance that both voices belong to one man converts every eerie beat into something worse, because the "conversation" is a researcher hallucinating a dead man in real time.  
- Vasiliev is characterized almost entirely by what he refuses to answer, and by the fact that he's already decided how the interview ends.  
- Escalating unreality that follows a logic — locked doors, closed curtains, infinite curtains, dilating space, then no space at all. The building degrades in the same direction the perceiver does.

**The horror:**

- You cannot un-perceive it. Amnestics don't work; death doesn't work; only ceasing to exist works. Knowledge is a permanent, non-transferable debt.  
- The unison phone call — two people in two "places," one of which is not a place, saying the same words at the same time.  
- The perceiver's realization that he is not the one seeing: "You look away and the patterns go away… if you look away, you'll stop seeing me."  
- Something crawled out, and the only witness will not describe it.  
- The last line is a countdown. Vasiliev is going in, and two Foundation staff are going to have to follow him, and no one has decided yet which two.

#### SCP-3935 — This Thing a Quiet Madness Made

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 3; Disruption Class Keneq, Risk Class Caution)

**Summary:** SCP-3935 is an extra-temporal, extra-spatial, non-Euclidean space beneath Salvation High School in Salvation, Indiana — reachable only through a collapsed sub-basement below the school's pool, some 25 meters down, through an antechamber containing a stone arch nobody built and nobody buried. The town of Salvation has been evacuated, fenced under a 2km quarantine, and the roads to nearby Eminence removed and rerouted; the cover story is a hazardous waste disaster. Exploration is currently forbidden, and any future attempt is restricted to Class-D. One containment note sits in the procedures without explanation and never gets one: *"It is currently believed that original inhabitants of Salvation are unable to reproduce. Deviation from this expectation is to be closely monitored."* Inscribed on the arch is a quatrain that exists nowhere else in literature: *"The way below winds deeper, longer, / unspeakable its patterns laid. / The lost forever damned to wander / this thing a quiet madness made."*

Addendum 3935.2 is the article's centerpiece: a UIU field report reconstructing, day by day, the week of June 18–23, 1976 at Salvation High. It reads like a haunting scheduled on a class timetable. Sunday: two tenth-graders hear "someone trying to speak under the ground" walking home from church. Monday: a student hears voices under the swimming pool; the pool lining is cracked; girls see "faceless things" in the second-floor bathroom mirrors instead of their reflections and *are not bothered by it*; a third voice mutters beneath the lunch announcements. Tuesday: the Indiana state flag floats three meters above the flagpole attached to nothing, the American flag is gone, and students see nine female figures hanging from the pole, which vanish the moment they're noticed. A freshman rolls his eyes back, sinks into the floor and reappears above a ceiling tile; nobody can identify him and his classmates insist it's a joke (a footnote: he is not in the town's population dossier). Students remember an unfamiliar classmate whose only describable feature was a purple satchel embroidered "Syncope." A custodian sees something standing at the bottom of the pool, watching him. Wednesday: two inches of water on every floor, and the principal declines to close the school; at 7:56 the entire building hears "hello" whispered in its right ear; the band's instruments make no sound but a small black human-shaped thing flickers in the corner, facing the wall. Thursday: a boy phases through a dodgeball and starts sinking into the floor, screaming for help, and *nobody is motivated enough to help him*; the school lifts a foot off its foundations and the vice principal sees "something small, with too many faces, grinning at him from underneath the building"; the boys' locker room is replaced by "something that screamed"; at the end of the day nine young women hang in the air 25 meters above the parking lot, tilted forward at 45 degrees, silently mouthing words, described by every witness as "ugly" and "unremarkable," and when they vanish at 3:00 the whole town hears a child's voice say "hello" from below them. Friday: the school is closed, the entire student body shows up anyway and none of them can say why. The building unlocks itself and the interior has gone non-Euclidean — walking toward the back means walking *down*. They glimpse the arch, are briefly entombed inside solid rock 50 meters outside the space, and reappear. Then each of them, separately and simultaneously, is alone in the school, finds "a doorway below a doorway," and sees three visions: a woman crouched over water with blood around her feet and her arms in it to the elbows; a farmhouse burning in a grove with nine figures hanging overhead; and a weeping woman digging in a field until her hands rot and fall apart. The black figure appears, says "hello," nine screaming women surround them, and they are all in their own homes. UIU dosed the entire town with the toxic experimental amnestic Chemical 110 and blamed factory vapors.

Addendum 3935.3, the initial Foundation search-and-rescue log, is where the article turns from folklore to descent. Agents Ellis, Porter and Haskel squeeze down a rock tunnel carved with drawings like a child's; Porter falls out of existence mid-sentence, screaming, and the other two can't turn around to look. The tunnel opens into a fogged chasm, and across a narrow stone bridge stands a structure carved from the rock — Salvation High School, its geometry wrong, its main entrance recognizable, and *another one below it, and another below that*, extending down past sight. Nine women with faces behind white hair appear over the bridge, teleport backward twice, then to the school's door; their faces become visible at a distance too great for the camera and both agents recoil. Inside, the halls have faces at the door glass, and when Ellis opens the auditorium the nine appear around him, contorted impossibly, and take him. Haskel runs, the nine hang motionless behind him, and he turns to find a small black featureless humanoid sitting upside down on the ceiling an inch from his face. It says: *"Hello."* A twenty-second tolling like a bell, then all three radios go dead — and then start returning, one at a time, at nine-minute intervals, for hours: Ellis babbling "wouldn't you like to just be… no more of the nine, we all would die"; Haskel — "this wall is like every other wall. This hall is like every other hall. Down and down and down and down, no no no no more please, no more down"; Haskel again, laughing, delivering the key: *"the tenth is down below, the tenth is the madness, woke it up, there are nine but the tenth is down below"*; Porter simply screaming; Haskel drowning ("You want us to all come down and be with you, down…" followed by wet choking and splashing); Ellis chanting "Fall forever\!" into rushing wind; Porter at the end — "It goes down forever. We're in here forever. Just more of the nine, more of Salvation, down forever and ever. None of them got out." Then an unidentified voice through Porter's radio: *"Hello."* Then weeping. Then static.

Addendum 3935.4 catalogues the town's ambient rot: a figure dressed as a UIU agent trying to steer people toward the high school; a black figure permanently in Agent Wills's peripheral vision; a dilapidated one-bedroom house in a grove with nine \[DATA EXPUNGED\] in the backyard, which the exploration team fled and could never find again; the nine women lurching toward agents in the dark before sliding into the earth; bodies — never more than nine — surfacing in a pond and sinking. Addendum 3935.5 supplies the human cause. Agent Aimes interviews Valerie Fletcher in 2002; she taught English at Salvation and her memory has been shredded by Chemical 110, so she keeps looping, losing the thread, and — chillingly — saying "hello?" into the gaps. Between the lapses, a story assembles itself: a tight, churchgoing town with no police and no crime; a girl who got pregnant out of wedlock, "quite a scandal," who sat on the bleachers hearing a knocking from underground, who asked about the locked room below the pool, who told Fletcher hello too many times at once, "like it was the only word she knew"; a girl found strung up in the woods, called a suicide, "one of the girls who did cheer," with a note in her hand — a drawing of a strange building on one side and a single word written over and over on the other, illegible because it was wet. And the memory Fletcher was told not to think about: a child floating backwards into a wall, pounding and screaming and suffocating, the whole town listening, and nobody being bothered by it. Aimes leaves, then turns back and shows her a photograph of the nine hanging women. She goes pale and short of breath, and gives the article its last line: *"Those are the, uh — that's the cheerleaders. There were, uh, well, let me think… there were ten of them, though, I think. Unless something happened to one of them, there were definitely ten."*

**Themes:**

- A town's respectability as the murder weapon; the cruelty is entirely ordinary before it is supernatural.  
- Complicity as an anomalous effect — the recurring, terrible detail that witnesses *are not bothered* by what they see.  
- Amnesia as institutional violence: Chemical 110 as both cover-up and second injury.  
- Grief that goes down instead of away — a hell built by an ordinary human misery.  
- The missing tenth: the victim who becomes the engine, and the nine who become her chorus.

**What makes it work:**

- The day-by-day structure. Building a haunting on a school week gives it inexorable rhythm and lets the surreality escalate against the most mundane possible backdrop (gym class, lunch announcements, band practice).  
- Three documents in three registers — UIU bureaucratese, a live exploration transcript, and a failing old woman's interview — that solve each other. None of them explains the anomaly; the reader assembles it.  
- The nine-minute intervals. A dead radio that keeps coming back on a schedule is a better structure for a torture sequence than any continuous scene could be.  
- The withheld tenth. The number nine is repeated so relentlessly that Fletcher's correction lands like a trapdoor, and the article ends there without a word of explanation.  
- Fletcher's "hello?" — the amnestic damage and the anomaly's signature word are the same sound, and the article never points at it.

**The horror:**

- The apathy effect. A boy sinks into the floor screaming for help and nobody can be bothered; a child suffocates inside a wall in front of the whole town and it "didn't bother" them. The town's real sin is dramatized as a supernatural symptom.  
- Infinite descent: a replica school with another school below it, and another, going down past the range of a torch, and Haskel begging "no more down."  
- "Hello," spoken by a small featureless thing hanging upside down, as the sound that precedes annihilation.  
- Salvation's inhabitants can't reproduce, and no one ever explains why that line is in the containment procedures.  
- The final reveal is not a monster but an arithmetic error, and it means a girl was hanged, or hanged herself, and the thing under the school woke up because of it.

#### SCP-4173 — The House on Hadley Hill

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 2/4173; Disruption Class Dark, Risk Class Caution)

**Summary:** SCP-4173 is a small access door in the cellar of an abandoned house near Mt. Zion, Georgia — the "House on Hadley Hill," named for the ridge behind it. Heavy rains in 1993 washed out the road, so anyone who wants to reach the house has to climb the steep face of Hadley's Hill. Access to the structure is forbidden; interaction requires the Site-94 research head's permission; anyone affected goes to the Site-94 infirmary. The anomaly is patient and conversational. Leave the door open for twenty minutes to an hour and a voice speaks from inside the wall beyond it — an old woman's voice. She asks who you are and *why you took so long to come*. She says she has trouble seeing. She asks to see your hand.

Put your hand through and there's a sharp jerk; withdraw the arm and the hand is gone, severed at the wrist as though by a sharp metal instrument. The voice thanks you and tells you not to worry, it will be replaced. Later, while you are asleep, a decaying elderly hand appears somewhere on or in your body — genetically unrelated to you. And here is the article's cruelest mechanic: **the hand's proximity to your severed wrist is directly proportional to how polite you were to her.** The hand works, wherever it is. It also keeps rotting — through putrescence to bare bone — and it goes on functioning after that, though subjects report feeling nothing in it past that point except "an occasional warm, wet, lingering sensation." (A footnote: decay can be slowed by keeping the hand in embalming fluid.) Refusing is worse. Anyone who engages the voice and then tries to leave without paying is dragged through the door — pulled by whichever part of the body is nearest, forced through an opening far too small for a person, "resulting in an immediate shattering, splitting, and spilling of the body as it is pulled through." Afterward the door closes itself.

Addendum 4173.1 gives the folklore, which the Foundation reports without endorsing. An elderly woman lived alone in the house. When the road washed out in the early '90s she couldn't get to town for food and had no family to check on her. Starving, she ate her own limbs. Having eaten her hands, she couldn't climb out of the cellar after falling in while hunting insects and vermin, and died there. Locals report hearing rubbing along the walls and a woman's voice from under the ground. The legend is called "Old Hadley."

Addendum 4173.2 is a single test that demonstrates every rule at once. D-94-322 is walked down into the dark cellar by Dr. Andrews, who assures him "there's nothing in that room right now that can hurt you" — a lie by verb tense. He waits thirty-four minutes, sees something move past the door, and then she starts talking: relieved, grandmotherly, apologetic about her eyes. "Come over here a little closer, I can't see you properly either." He refuses, profanely — "Get fucked bitch, I'm not getting my hands anywhere near this spooky-ass hole in the wall" — and Andrews, flatly, tells him he has to, because they have never recovered anyone who refused. He complies, cursing. And the voice changes: *"Oh, I can see you now… It has been so long since someone came, and with a gift as well? Sweetling, you're so kind. So kind to me."* Then, without transition: *"It's so dark and lonesome, and I've just been so hungry for such a long time… I'll take it from you — your gift. You're such a rude little shit, you don't deserve it. You've had it for too long, and you don't deserve it. It's mine now. Mine for my belly."* A wet ripping sound. He passes out. And she keeps talking to him while he's unconscious: *"Don't worry, you ungrateful whore child. Don't worry. I'll fill you up too. You can have mine. I'll give it to you. Sweet boy. Good boy. Fill you up."* The door closes.

He is extracted and stabilized. Two days later a large pustulous growth emerges from the right side of his head; he panics on seeing it, the growth begins pressing on his brain, and he dies en route to trauma care. The autopsy finds that the growth is a decaying elderly hand that has appeared *between his brain and his skull*, cracking the skull open from inside. It went where his rudeness sent it. And the file's last sentence: the hand went on clutching and clawing at his brain for several hours after death.

**Themes:**

- Manners as a load-bearing supernatural law — courtesy is the only currency, and the exchange rate is your skull.  
- Abandonment and its debts: an old woman left to starve, and a rural landscape that let it happen.  
- The obligate transaction — you can decline, but declining costs more than accepting.  
- Ordinary rural neglect (a washed-out road, no family, no one checking) as the true origin of a monster.  
- The Foundation's casual cruelty: Dr. Andrews knows the rules and sends a man in anyway, telling him only as much as gets him to comply.

**What makes it work:**

- The politeness mechanic is a genuinely original piece of anomaly design — it makes the reader anxious about *diction*, and it converts an ordinary exploration log into a scene where every line of dialogue is a dice roll.  
- The voice's tonal turn is the whole article. Two paragraphs of tender gratitude collapsing into "you ungrateful whore child" in a single breath, with the endearments still attached.  
- The folklore addendum is short, unverified, and does all the emotional work the clinical sections refuse to do — the Foundation never confirms it, which keeps it a story people tell.  
- The horror is withheld for two days after the scene ends, so the payoff lands in an autopsy report rather than a scream.  
- "Except for an occasional warm, wet, lingering sensation" — one clause, buried in the Description, implying that something is doing something to the hand wherever it originally went.

**The horror:**

- Body-integrity violation with no undo: a foreign, rotting hand grafted somewhere on or *inside* you, permanently, functional, decaying past bone and still moving.  
- The punishment for refusal — being pulled through a pet-door-sized opening by whatever part of you is closest.  
- The placement rule, which means D-94-322's death sentence was passed the moment he swore at her, and he never knew he was being graded.  
- The last line: a hand clawing at a brain for hours after the brain's owner is dead.

#### SCP-4176 — taxes

**Object class:** Keter. Co-authored with Uncle Nicolini. Epigraph: *"Don't fuck with the IRS."*

**Summary:** SCP-4176 is a yearly phenomenon at the Adna Post Office in Adna, Washington, usually in the last week of January. An unmarked, customized ICBM launches from somewhere in the Pacific — a different spot every year — arcs toward the unincorporated territory of Adna, and lands in the field in front of the post office. Foundation cover story: arms tests. Embedded agents administer amnestics. No civilian has ever been hurt. Each missile carries secured hatches spray-painted with "taxes for (year) fiscal year" and "to: internal revenue service, washington usa" — with a footnote noting that "revenue" has never once been spelled correctly, though many different misspellings have appeared. Inside the hatches are objects of enormous monetary value, which the Foundation simply keeps as assets.

The article's substance is the payment ledger, and it works as escalating absurdist poetry. 2010: a deed to a Manhattan parking garage, 50kg of 16th-century gold coins, fifteen sable coats, and 75% of a well-preserved mammoth. 2011: Munch's *The Scream*, verified genuine — and five wrapped Publix BLT submarine sandwiches. 2012: 75kg of radiant-cut rubies, a 1952 Topps Mickey Mantle, a Hendrix-signed 1968 Stratocaster, five boxes of Gurkha Black Dragon cigars. 2013: a Rembrandt, 693 liters of the amnestic Y-909, and three mint first-edition Black Lotuses. 2014: twenty humanoid figurines with a certificate of authenticity signed by Dr. Wondertainment, another 25% of a mammoth (moderately preserved this time), and one framed image of a guinea pig chewing a carrot. 2015: a single capsule of SCP-500. 2016: a golden Buddha, a wooden chalice, a portrait of John D. Rockefeller, **the Voyager 1 space probe**, eighteen goats, the Coca-Cola recipe, and one ornate gold key to an unknown lock.

Then the addendum: in 2017 the event failed to occur on schedule. Observation continued through February and March; the missile finally arrived in April, carrying a deed to Tojo Una Una Island and 800 shares of Amazon stock. The message painted on this one was different: *"hello yes pls accept as payment for the tax of 2017 fiscal year thank u. sorry for is late, dado go 2 vacation w/ hamster."* The article ends there. dado has been diligently, anonymously, and by intercontinental ballistic missile paying his income taxes for eight years, and the only reason he was ever late is that he took a holiday with his hamster.

**Themes:**

- Overwhelming power in the service of scrupulous civic obedience.  
- Bureaucratic literalism — an entity that has correctly identified filing deadlines and delivery addresses and gotten every other detail catastrophically wrong.  
- The anticlimactic reveal: a Keter-class annual ICBM strike whose author is sweetly apologetic.  
- Value as noise — a Rembrandt and five BLTs listed as equivalent line items.

**What makes it work:**

- The list format is the joke. Each year's manifest ends on something that punctures the grandeur — five sandwiches, a guinea pig photo, eighteen goats — and the deadpan tabular presentation refuses to acknowledge it.  
- The article withholds dado's name until the very last line, so the whole piece retroactively becomes a character sketch.  
- "sorry for is late, dado go 2 vacation w/ hamster" is a perfect closing beat: it explains the anomaly, characterizes the author, and reveals that a missile schedule is subject to one man's vacation plans.  
- Voyager 1 sitting mid-list, unremarked, is the best single item on the page.

**The horror:**

- Barely present, and deliberately so — but the containment procedures quietly admit the Foundation cannot stop the launches, only lie about them and pocket the proceeds.  
- The launch site changes every year and is never located. Someone has repeated, undetectable access to intercontinental ballistic missiles.  
- The Foundation's response to an uncontainable annual weapons event is to take the money.

#### SCP-4390 — A Bad Maze

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 2/4390; Disruption Class Vlam, Risk Class Notice)

**Summary:** SCP-4390 is an extra-spatial region under Giordano's Italian Eatery on the west side of Atlanta, entered through a fake walk-in refrigerator in the basement. Beyond it is a cavernous space holding an enormous shifting stone labyrinth overgrown with plants and populated by fauna that resemble nothing on Earth. The walls run up at least twenty meters, but climbing doesn't work: ascend and you shrink exponentially until you're too small to go higher; descend and it reverses. The maze is explicitly built as a *game*. Intelligent entities are stationed inside it to pose challenges, and many will offer wagers — you can bet anything that could be called yours, including possessions, debts, bodily functions and personality traits, against an anomalous object or trait of equal value. Possessions carry out; body parts and traits are restored on exit. Die inside and you become a spectral phantom that follows your party, able to pass through walls but barred from solving puzzles or answering riddles (a footnote clarifies phantoms usually can't speak, but are "not bound from pantomime or charades"). When the party finishes the maze or is wiped out, everyone reappears at the entrance restored to flesh — but a wiped party comes back stripped of every possession, including their clothes. Exploration is run jointly with representatives of Dr. Wondertainment.

Addendum 4390.1 explains why. Sebastian Juneaux Malthuzan Hermano y Konquistadito Vanabulous Extraordinariorion Frank Fitchingsley, marketing director for Dr. Wondertainment, materializes in Assistant Director Duncan's shower and then sits for a formal interview to explain a succession crisis. Dr. Wondertainment is a hereditary title; the last holder, Isabel, has disappeared under mysterious circumstances, and rather than pass to her presumed successor — her distant cousin Yancy diPettito-Cortez Wondertainment — the board reinstalled a *previous* Wondertainment, Bertrand, as caretaker while Isabel is searched for. Yancy took this as a mortal insult, splintered off, renamed himself **Professor Amazementopia**, and set up under a pizza place to build a competing product: "The Maze 2: The Game." (There was never a Maze 1\. "This is the sort of shit I'm talking about.") Fitchingsley's assessment of Yancy is that he doesn't understand toymaking — "he's more like a kid who builds a big thing out of Lego and then doesn't want anyone else to play with it." The favor: Yancy stole a filing cabinet of financial records, not realizing it also contains the board's folder of mutually incriminating nude photographs, kept as insurance ("It's one thing to threaten a man with a lawsuit, and another to threaten him with exposing his dingle dangle"). Wondertainment can't retrieve it themselves without tipping Yancy off that it's valuable, so they want the Foundation as their bluff. Duncan can keep everything else in the maze. He agrees. Fitchingsley asks whether everyone's up to date on vaccinations — "tetanus, measles, ebola" — and the log ends.

Addendum 4390.2 is the first exploration, and a total rout. MTF Atlanta-2 "Fucking Traffic" (Franko, Vice, Liter) walk in and meet **The Face**, an entity that appears on any wall and is cursed to SHOUT ALL ANNOUNCEMENTS to maintain excitement. It explains the rules, admits the only exit is death, insists on singing the Maze Song, is insulted, and turns hostile forever. Vice is startled by The Face's next appearance, falls into a pit, and dies. Liter tries to jump the gap, dies. Franko solves the "very difficult tree puzzle" by pushing over a tree, reaches a witch with a cauldron of random brews, drinks one and gains eyes that can see the entire maze and its exit, then drinks a second hoping for super speed and explodes in a shower of bananas. All three reappear naked at the entrance. "WOW YOU GUYS ARE ASS."

Addendum 4390.3 is the second run, with Cairo and Dance added, and it's a small comic epic. They meet Dominican Joe, an arachnoid-reptilian Australian merchant ("me mum was a crawly hog, and me dad was a snatch-gobbler") selling lightning bullets, a hat that protects from falling objects, and an item best left summarized, in a currency of "scraps of Na'ath" made from the sail of Midas's ship; unable to pay, Liter declines to trade his toes and is given a brass coin that will summon the shop from any crack in a wall. They meet The Dreamer, a "Catagan" ("like a sphinx, but fewer component parts" — a sphinx wasn't in the budget), who poses three riddles with five lives at stake. Cairo dispatches the first two easily (the doctor is the mother; an island and the letter T are both in the middle of water), muffs the third, and is eaten; Liter, of all people, produces "a towel." Cairo reappears as a ghost through the creature's flank. They hit a two-guards puzzle with Señor Honestidad and Señor Deshonestidad, which Liter — the only person present with any Spanish — comprehensively ruins by asking both of them directly which way to go, after first inquiring whether there is a party in his pants and they are invited.

Then they find Yancy himself, lost in his own maze with a map in his hand, in a bathrobe and a star-spangled top hat, having created The Face ("Eugene") and now stuck inside his own creation. He admits he's racing them to the same folder to post it on his Wordpress blog. A chase follows: scythes, a collapsing bridge, a stone column that kills Franko ("WHOOPSY POOPSY"), and a corridor of arrow slits shaped like The Face's mouth firing *cardboard arrows* — Yancy shouting from an adjacent passage that "not everyone has billion dollar budgets… these unrealistic expectations on toy makers really sour the pudding." Midway through, Yancy flings open a door and the team steps out somewhere else entirely: a burned mountain range under a dark sky, dead trees, the charred husks of enormous insectoid creatures, and a tower rising impossibly high into the clouds. The temperature spikes, something chatters all around them, the sky above the tower turns orange, and a kilometer-wide pillar of flame arcs down. They scramble back through the door and slam it, and the article never explains a single thing about it.

At the center, Vice grabs the folder just as The Face assembles a stone golem out of the courtyard wall; Liter — who has quietly sold his toes to Dominican Joe off-screen for the lightning bullets — hobbles forward and blows the golem apart. Confetti lights and airhorns. Then Fitchingsley steps through a door drawn in midair with Bertrand Wondertainment, who calmly tells Yancy he's built a death trap, not a toy, and asks him to come home. Yancy refuses — "I'm not going home, dad. I'm Professor Amazementopia, and I'm going to be the best goddamn toymaker in the fucking omniverse" — and vanishes. Bertrand takes the folder, thanks them, tells Fitchingsley to stop photographing his johnson, and asks them to be patient with Yancy: "He means well, I believe. He's just an asshole." He leaves. Liter starts to ask how they get out. A stone disc the size of the courtyard falls on all of them, The Face on its underside — **"EAT MY ASS, CHUMPS."** They reappear at the entrance, naked and stripped of everything, and Vice says: "Fuck."

**Themes:**

- Bad art made by a wounded ego; the maze is genuinely dangerous *and* genuinely shoddy, and both facts are Yancy's fault.  
- Inheritance, legitimacy and family grievance played as farce (with a real father-son wound underneath).  
- Play with fatal stakes — a game where death is a respawn mechanic and the only penalty is your clothes.  
- Institutional pragmatism: the Foundation runs errands for a toy company because there's loot in it.  
- A glimpse of something enormous and grim just past a door, which nobody follows up on.

**What makes it work:**

- The Face is one of the wiki's great comic creations — an announcer entity that is contractually obliged to shout, has feelings, and holds a grudge for the entire runtime, culminating in a murder that is purely spite.  
- Genuine RPG structure — merchant, riddle-guardian, two-guards puzzle, chase, boss — deflated at every station by characters who treat it as an annoying work assignment.  
- Liter is a superbly constructed idiot: he blows the logic puzzle, wins the riddle contest by accident, and quietly sells his toes to buy the weapon that saves everyone.  
- Yancy is funny and then, briefly, sad. Bertrand's "let's go home" and Yancy's "I'm not going home, *dad*" reframe the entire farce as a tantrum with real hurt in it.  
- The burnt world behind the door is a masterful piece of unexplained furniture — thirty seconds of authentic cosmic dread dropped into a comedy and never mentioned again.

**The horror:**

- Mostly absent by design, but the wagering economy is quietly monstrous: an entity in a maze can buy your toes, your bodily functions, or your personality traits, and men do trade them.  
- Death is cheap and repeatable, which means the maze can kill you as many times as it wants and the only thing you lose is dignity — a joke that is also a description of hell.  
- The door to the burned world: charred insect corpses the size of buildings, a tower in the clouds, chattering closing in, and a kilometer of fire coming down. Yancy's only reaction is to run.  
- The ending. They win, they're congratulated, they're thanked by name — and then a wall drops on them out of pure malice.

#### SCP-4444 — Bush v. Gore

**Object class:** Esoteric — **Ticonderoga** (Level 4; Disruption Class Vlam, Risk Class Warning). The Ticonderoga class was created by the Classification Committee on a 9-2 vote specifically for this object.

**Summary:** SCP-4444 is an incorporeal extradimensional entity that has been sharing the body of former Vice President Al Gore since late 1998\. Invisible to the naked eye, under infrared it is a meter-long translucent hexagonal pyramid protruding from the back of Gore's head, covered in glowing alien glyphs and pierced by six triangular vents. It can render Gore incorporeal, letting him walk through any barrier; no Foundation technology can restrain it. It calls itself Garber Gore, speaks by borrowing Gore's voice in fractured word-salad, and belongs to a vast collective of similar conical entities that travel from world to world looking for sentients to cohabit — a process that usually ends with the host planet dying of greenhouse failure, since the entities vent carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide when exposed to sunlight. The collective will not arrive until one of their number occupies the most powerful being on the planet. Garber picked wrong: a translation error led it to believe "Vice" implied superiority, so it lodged itself in the Vice President. Unable to leave, it decided the fix was simple — get Gore elected President in 2000\. Because the entity is uncontainable but placid, and because thwarting it provokes breaches, containment is redefined as management: constant discreet surveillance including inside Gore's home, standing crisis negotiators on-site, a joint Foundation/UIU security detail, no apprehension attempts, no removal attempts, and limits on Gore's public appearances with his consent.

Addendum 4444.1 is the discovery: two UIU agents on Clinton's detail overhearing a call between the President and DNC chair Joe Andrew about Gore announcing a run and mentioning "some sort of extra-terrestrial living in his brain" that "wants to run for president so it can sodomize the world." Clinton suggests reefer; Andrew reports Gore has been sitting up all night trying on hats and walking through closed doors to fetch Cokes from the fridge "like some sort of ghostly beverage"; Clinton concedes, "that's probably not the reefer, then." Addendum 4444.2 is the interview, a comic masterpiece of two registers — Gore's genial folksy deflection ("I still put my pants on like the other guys, one leg at a time" / "you actually seemed to phase into your pants the other day") interleaved with Garber breaking through in bursts of glossolalia ("Nefariousness\! Fallacy uptake the nonsense. Garber Gore accounted"), with Gore cheerfully translating for him. Gore describes the merger honestly: "Sometimes it's hard to tell where the line between Al and Garber is… I hadn't actually considered running or anything. But Garber is very persuasive." He then explains, politely, that containment is not on the table, because they can float out through the wall whenever they like.

Addendum 4444.3 puts the Foundation and the federal government in one room — FBI Director Freeh, UIU Director Bishop, Assistant Director Howard Dean, General Roland, and for the Foundation, Sophia Light, Lachlan Cairns, Jeremiah Cimmerian, Kain Pathos Crow (a talking dog, noted mid-meeting by a startled FBI scientist) and Alto Clef, who characterizes the entity as "a motherfucker" that exists across parallel dimensions, probably can't see, and whose vents are "probably just for decoration, or something." Crow proposes his Incorporeal Entity Vacuum Chamber; Clef notes the downside is that non-ghosts placed in it explode. Light lays out the real strategy: the entities believe they are weaker than corporeal beings, so if Gore can be made *unelectable*, the collective may conclude Earth is too dangerous and pass it by, and Garber may leave on its own. Rigging the election outright is rejected as too likely to expose the Foundation. When Cimmerian says they'll need to contact Governor Bush's campaign, Freeh delivers the news that Bush was shot in a hunting accident the previous night and is brain-dead, awaiting removal from a ventilator. Light asks whether the body still works. Then: "I think I know how we're going to oppose the Vice President's campaign." Director Cairns: "Oh no you fucking don't."

Addendum 4444.4 is Cairns's official complaint form, redacted by the personnel department into a masterpiece of bracketed euphemism (\[EXPLETIVE\], \[OBSCENE METAPHOR\], \[DEROGATORY NAME FOR A POLITICIAN\], \[CREATIVE USE OF SLANG, METAPHOR, ALLUSION AND EUPHEMISM APPROXIMATING OBSCENITY\]) — he was body-hopped into Bush's corpse ("I let you do the monkey thing because it was funny, but this is not"), can't defecate without a pump, and Lament keeps telling him he looks like a chimpanzee. Addendum 4444.5 confirms the mechanism: Bush's body was collected and allowed to expire, SCP-8586 was shot in the head, and its consciousness transferred into the Governor. Then come the discarded disinformation proposals — poisoning a third-world town's water (rejected: no convincing candidates), doctored images of Gore with a pig (rejected: photo-editing insufficient), claiming Gore believes he's spoken with an alien (rejected: too close to the truth) — and the one that was approved 5-4: a secret love triangle between Gore, Clinton, and a treble saxophone.

Addendum 4444.6, "The September Debate," is the article's showpiece. A Foundation-controlled town hall in Plainfield, Indiana, moderated by Jim Lehrer, broadcast on a three-minute delay. Gore/Garber begins competently and then dissolves mid-sentence into "the interesting compliances whereabout matter in complacencies and troglodyte hernias," asking America to elect "the best ambivalent vicarious corncrake caliper available for the job." Cairns, delighted, goes wildly off-script as Bush — bragging about putting "the downtrodden and disenfranchised in the chair," announcing that his White House will bar communists and saxophonists, adhering to "the church of brass, and brass alone," and adding: "I know that seems kind of out of nowhere, but take my word for it — this will make a whole lot more sense in about 26 years." A planted agent ambushes Gore with the saxophone story and photographs; the crowd riots; Assistant Director Moose is trapped trying to reach the stage; and Garber, panicking, casts an actual spell — "Gregarious enchant the manatee\! Vex papyrus in the dust\! Conflagration imitate the date, and diphosphorus cacophony. I am a serious candidate gosh darn it" — elongating members of the audience into shapes that block the exits, then bolting through a wall and running unimpeded to a hayloft in western Ohio, where he hides for three days. Task forces descend through the ceiling on grapples. One NBC affiliate out of Evansville broadcasts the whole thing; Class A amnestics are deployed to every household that saw it. Cairns is disciplined by the Ethics Committee and, along with Garber, agrees to strict rules for the October debates. (A footnote: Garber apologized for "casting a dark spell" and promised it wouldn't happen again.)

Addendum 4444.7 collects the banned campaign ads — the cow methane spot, in which Gore tapes a plastic bag to a cow's rear end and asks America to "tape up the anuses of your cows" because "some creatures out there can double, or quintuple that output. In a day. And some of those creatures might be living here, right on Earth, very soon," signing off "My name is Al Gore, and fledgling nocturne annihilism fellow Garber Gore, and we want to tape American bags over American cow anuses"; and a slow zoom on a sleeping cat that becomes an appeal to shave the back of your head. "Shave em, buster." Addendum 4444.8 handles the Global Occult Coalition, which calls the UIU with a photo of the Vice President floating out of a second-story wall and gets stonewalled by Agent Montoya into admitting they found it on the internet — bracketed by a list of circulating conspiracy theories that terminates in "Vice President Al Gore is the ghost of an alien Bigfoot."

Addendum 4444.9 is election night. Networks call Florida for Gore; Foundation agents try and fail to disrupt voting; and at 8pm the Overseers authorize contingency **RED VELVET** — assassinate the President-elect in a hotel whose basement is already being filled with gas, cover it as a gas leak, with vacuum nullifiers now installed near all major world leaders to stop a replacement entity, and doomsday preparations if it fails. Freeh: "I'm not going to pretend like we haven't iced public officials before, but on the night they're elected President?" O5-2: "We prepare for doomsday. We're already watching the skies." Mid-call, Light sees the television — Florida is swinging back — and hangs up: "oh shit, we're filling the basement of that hotel with gas — gotta go\!" After the recount, Bush (Cairns) wins despite losing the popular vote, and neither Gore nor Garber does anything anomalous at all. Gore's concession letter to Bush is the article's final grace note: *"Sometimes you get in your head the way you think things should be, and you forget that just thinking something doesn't make it real… For a time, I would've thought the stars themselves were behind me."* He signs off saying he's going to travel, and that the cow ad got him thinking about how much greenhouse gas America produces — "I hope you address it. I think I'm going to address it, too." Signed *Al, and Garber*. Two codas follow: a voicemail from an unidentified caller in an Atlantic City payphone, speaking in a mangled dialect that is unmistakably Trump, gloating that "Shrubbery Junior am the crown-snag at lasters"; and an unexplained ad that aired once in Gainesville in 2007, in which Gore in head-to-toe denim sits on a hill above the sea, something enormous falls out of the sky behind him, and he says: *"I'm Al Gore, and I want to be your last American President."*

**Themes:**

- Real history rewritten as containment failure — the 2000 election as a covert species-survival operation.  
- Democracy as a thing the Foundation manipulates while insisting it isn't omnipotent ("If you think this is the first time we've fucked with your elections, I've got some bad news for you there bub").  
- Bureaucratic invention under pressure: the Ticonderoga class as an admission of impotence dressed up as policy.  
- The genuinely warm portrait of Al Gore, who is patient, decent, and entirely aware there's an alien in his head.  
- Climate catastrophe played first as a punchline and then, in the concession letter, straight.

**What makes it work:**

- Two voices in one mouth. The Gore/Garber duet — folksy Southern courtesy interrupted by alien glossolalia and then politely translated — is a sustained comic device the article never wears out.  
- Real events are used as the punchline rather than the premise: Florida, the recount, the popular-vote split, "strategery," the saxophone, and Gore's climate career all become consequences of the plot.  
- Cairns's redacted complaint form is a formal joke that also does character work — the bracketed euphemisms are funnier than obscenity would be.  
- The article knows exactly when to stop being funny. RED VELVET is played completely straight, and the concession letter is sincere.  
- The final two addenda withhold their punchlines: neither the payphone caller nor the 2007 ad is explained, and both are better for it.

**The horror:**

- RED VELVET. A room of officials calmly deciding to murder the President-elect on election night and pump a hotel basement full of gas, with a doomsday plan behind it — and being interrupted by good news mid-sentence.  
- Body theft as routine procedure: a brain-dead governor harvested and reoccupied, with the occupant's chief complaint being that the plumbing doesn't work.  
- Garber's collective. This is a *scout*. Something is waiting for the signal, and the plan for stopping it was to lose an election.  
- "I want to be your last American President," spoken by a man in denim while something enormous falls out of the sky behind him, in an ad nobody made.

#### SCP-4485 — Such Black Light

**Object class:** Initially Euclid-Red / Euclid (Level 3, Keneq, Warning) in the 1967 documents; in the Level 6 Cosmic Top Secret primary document, Containment Class **esoteric**, Secondary Class **Apollyon**, Disruption Class Amida, Risk Class Critical. Co-authored with Woedenaz.

**Summary:** SCP-4485 is not presented as a file. It is presented as a **mandatory comprehension orientation**, and the reader is enrolled in it. The landing page demands an employee alias and ID, announces "EYE TRACKING ACTIVATED. ACTIVITY WILL BE MONITORED. DO NOT PROCEED WITHOUT READING IN FULL," and explains that documents are released in chronological order and your direct supervisor will approve each stage. Part 1 of 8 opens with a remote terminal message from Director Lloyd Michaelson of Site-12, who is "pleased to see you are complying" and would have been "most displeased to have to terminate one of our most well respected and senior researchers."

The 1967 documents establish the object. SCP-4485 is a small, worn, leather-bound journal by the Alsatian dadaist Jean "Hans" Arp, his name pressed into the back cover; inside the front cover is the phrase **"gott ist ohne zweck"** — *god is without purpose* — under a crude drawing of a fish. Anyone who reads it without training in formal logic loses the ability to distinguish truth from falsehood, and that inability physically deforms reality around them. The containment procedures are the article's first great joke and its first real threat: access requires certified mastery of the Three Laws of Thought, Schopenhauer's fourfold root, Boole, Russell and Whitehead's *Principia*, the ability to independently reason Gödel's proof, Tarski's adaptations of identity, and fluency in intuitionistic, paraconsistent, three-valued, modal and fuzzy logic — "in short, subjects must have an absolute mastery, at the highest level achievable by human beings." Document Two is Agent Fredrick Norman's field report from the German town of Bramund, and it is one of the most vivid pieces of prose in the piece: hospital patients who no longer recognize their own names and call themselves and each other "hansarp"; two formerly hostile Church of the Broken God zealots gone placid, saying only that "god is already here," chattering like animals; a column of greyscale and orange rising out of the town's borders with geometric shapes and unknown alphabets moving in it; a road where men stop, declare themselves lost, and walk off at ninety degrees into the fog; color draining out of skin and equipment as they cross the town line. At the epicenter, in the ruins of a demolished anartist library, a ring of living human beings twisted into unnatural shapes serve as components of a machine that raises and drops a mass of barbed steel, concrete and human remains onto a platform, while the ring dances and sings in harmony with it and each pulped body is flung upward into a forming image in the sky "that defied understanding." Norman's team kills everyone and burns the ruin. At the machine's center they find a living man with the skin of his face peeled back and suspended from his skull on wires, still breathing. Asked his name, he says "hansarp." Asked what happened, he hands over the book. Asked what it is, he says it is god. He was Boris Aldermann, the regional Church of the Broken God leader. They could not remove his body from the machine, and Norman believes it is still there.

Part 2 supplies the group-of-interest profile — "HANSARP," origin unknown, active across Europe, producing transient anomalous art pieces and machines tagged in orange spray paint and signed with a crude vertical fish, all of which vanish shortly after being noticed — and a catalogue of recovered artifacts that are pure dadaist menace: a watercolor of an artist painting a watercolor with its head on its back, which made viewers see duplicates of themselves and duplicates of the duplicates, then dissolved into colored water; a pencil sketch of three children eating each other's feet, which left everyone in a Munich courthouse unable to pass through doorways; a clockwork machine producing a paper fish every sixteen minutes bearing a racial epithet that reads as "what will you do?" to the group it targets; a green arrow on a lamp post that induces severe anxiety and self-loathing and *spreads to anyone who interacts with the afflicted* (the lamp post was sandblasted into shavings; the shavings retained the effect); and one entry redacted entirely because the location and description are themselves memetic — the first artifact to work without being physically present.

Document Five is the hinge: a three-way call in 1983 between Dr. Viktor Jates (mathematics), Dr. Isaac Harmon (containment), and Dr. Leonard Fitzwell (GOI liaison). The Critic — the loose figurehead of Are We Cool Yet? — has *contacted the Foundation to investigate itself*, because a hundred-person AWCY? studio near Munich walked out one day leaving all their belongings behind. HANSARP has been working on disillusioned anartists: people who chase "cool" as a state of synthesis with their own art and can never reach it. And then the reframe that reorganizes the whole article: **the art was never anomalous.** The book does nothing in containment. The pieces do nothing in containment. What is anomalous is what happens inside a person who encounters weaponized meaninglessness. Researchers at Site-35 studying the book grew detached, started asking strange questions; those with a history of strong convictions became agitated and violent; the rest went blank. Then the psychologists who spoke to them changed too. "Talking to these people changes the way you see the world, and once you've become like they are, the world changes to fit what you see. Madness." HANSARP is not recruited, it is *contracted* — "like a disease. Like a virus. You see it and it works its way into your soul and by the time you realize what has happened to you, you're HANSARP." The only people immune were Jates's mathematicians, trained to derive truth by formula. "There aren't enough, Isaac. We would need an army of mathematicians, and that just — it doesn't exist." The call ends with Fitzwell, who has been reading the book in his study for a month, asking Jates to tell him truthfully whether he has said anything that didn't make sense. Jates answers: "Just once. Sideways eyes." Fitzwell's reply begins coherently and comes apart mid-sentence into redacted glyph-text. He apologizes. He says goodnight. Harmon is already handling it.

The framing story runs in parallel and eats the file. Michaelson's terminal messages start out coldly bureaucratic — asked "what does 'comprehension' mean?", he answers "a depressingly common question… if you need to ask this question, you likely will not pass"; asked what failure means: "You will be terminated." Then he steps away, and **Dr. Gary Lark** takes over the terminal. Lark is friendly. Lark offers to help you cheat. "Don't worry about it, okay. They won't know. Just give me a little time. And especially do not worry about Lloyd. I asked to make sure he doesn't come back." He warns you only about the survey, which "goes straight to the Reason Council." The document he serves is a recovered HANSARP recruiting text — "Master Master\! Confirm for me that which I already know\!… Data data. Metal and prongs… We all know it, we all HANSARP" — and then the Comprehension Assessment, an interactive survey demanding your position and title, what object is directly to your left, how you feel, whether you know where you are, and — critically — **"NAME THAT WHICH YOU TRUST MOST,"** a text field which, whatever keys you press, types **THE FOUNDATION**. By Part "▓▓ of 8," Lark's header has been corrupted and his message has stopped pretending: *"I don't have much longer here. The sideways eyes lift their fins to the sky and sink into the abyss. It calls for me and I ignore it. Soon it will have me. And you. And nothing, if you ask me. R i d i c u l o u s\!"* The document he opens announces itself as the scanned diary of Jean Arp — and instead the viewer flashes UNAUTHORIZED ACCESS DETECTED, ERROR, then "Authorization approved. Thank you, ▓▓▓▓HANSARP. ⿂⿂⿂," and delivers pages of dada directly to the reader: *"understanding is searching for an empty cup from which to drink air… in contradiction we find all that we are… no it does not matter and thus matter loses matter… you do hide / you don't taste / you are pith… i am HANSARP. we are HANSARP."*

The final document is the present-day Level 6 file, and it is a portrait of an institution that has destroyed itself to survive. SCP-4485 and everyone and everything associated with HANSARP are held in the **Scranton-Jates Supra-Cultural Dissociation Engine** at DEEPWELL-9, beneath the former Site-35 at the Isar II nuclear plant near Landshut — a shaft of stacked concrete rings, each holding up to two hundred pacified humans in cryogenic stasis plus artifact lockers, wrapped in titanium and ballistic nanofiber rated for a direct 5-megaton surface hit, around a core (formerly its own SCP) that dissociates cognition from psychological response and functions as a time sink at one day inside to forty-six days outside. Entering the active core causes immediate or progressive brain death. The site director is Viktor Jates. To contain this, the Foundation has instituted compulsory logical "Orientation" of every employee at every level, with failure meaning termination *and* containment in the Engine — and, on an 11-2 O5 vote, **dissolved the Ethics Committee** as "unnecessary and actively detrimental," with all its members deemed unfit and possibly tainted. Ninety-two percent of European staff are considered at risk; over three hundred sites are closed; Europe is "effectively lost to us."

Dr. Inan Yoss's report to the O5 Council gives the final theory. SCP-4485 is the seed of a self-propagating cultural consciousness that self-realized shortly after Arp's death in 1966; Arp was not anomalous and neither is the book. "SCP-4485 does not change people; people are changed upon reading SCP-4485… SCP-4485 is like a key, perfectly randomly shaped to fit a lock within the human psyche that demands order. Once unlocked, the mind begins to change itself, and the world around that mind follows." **SCP-4485-B** instances are the apathetic — the un-ideological, who go blank, ask nonsensical questions, propagate by sight and sound, and grow incorporeal and indistinct; en masse they dissolve logic itself in an area on an exponential curve. **SCP-4485-A** instances are the rejectors — people with strong convictions who become monomaniacal, then violent, then encrust into levitating calcium crystal — and they exist to manufacture more B instances. In Yoss's image, "If SCP-4485-B instances are the consciousness of HANSARP trying to free itself from the hull of its humanity with a blender, SCP-4485-A instances are the blades." Together they are a metaconstruct with one purpose: to annihilate reason. And it hasn't been contained. Only the people who could be located inside locked sites are in the Engine; 65% of security and janitorial staff, everyone on leave, everyone in transit — thousands of functional super-intelligences — are unaccounted for. The B instances are more potent in proximity, and the Foundation has put a thousand of them in a bubble the size of an apartment building. Yoss's closing figure is the article's thesis: *"In our attempts to save ourselves we have built a bomb filled to the brim with the most potent of payloads. We have armed it with a fuse that logically cannot be lit, and are hoping to keep it safe from an intelligence that does not care whether the facts say it can or cannot."* Attached is a photograph recovered from the last site they burned: a sluice gate servicing DEEPWELL-9, taken by no one. **They know.**

**Themes:**

- Dada as an actual weapon — meaninglessness as a solvent for the mind's demand that things cohere.  
- Apathy and zealotry as the same disease, mutually manufacturing each other.  
- The Foundation destroying everything it claims to protect in order to survive: dissolving Ethics, terminating staff for insufficient rationality, freezing its own people.  
- Two failed absolutes reflected in each other — the Church of the Broken God's god that cannot be rebuilt, and Are We Cool Yet?'s "cool" that cannot be reached. "If you break down the structures that determine what 'broken' is, or what 'cool' is, what are you left with?"  
- Ideas as pathogens, and the reader as the next carrier.

**What makes it work:**

- The form is the anomaly. This is not a document about a cognitohazard; it is a compulsory training module with your name in it, an eye-tracking warning, and a supervisor grading your comprehension — and by Part 3 the supervisor has been replaced by something wearing his login.  
- The reveal that the artifacts were never anomalous is a genuine structural rug-pull. Everything read as containment up to that point retroactively becomes transmission.  
- The absurd containment procedures — a wall of real logicians and real philosophy — are simultaneously the funniest thing in the article and its most despairing: the only defense against nonsense is a credential almost nobody can earn, and "there aren't enough."  
- Norman's Bramund report is the piece's engine of dread, and it's written in restrained period military prose that never editorializes about a man whose face has been peeled off his skull and wired open.  
- The survey's trust field. Whatever you type, it types THE FOUNDATION — a small, silent, deeply nasty gesture that demonstrates the article's whole thesis with no words at all.  
- "Sideways eyes" as a shibboleth: two words the reader watches a man discover he has already said.

**The horror:**

- The Bramund machine — a pile-driver crewed by ecstatic living components, pulping volunteers into an image forming in the sky, and a man wired open at its heart who says the book is god.  
- Infection through conversation. Talking to an affected person affects you; the psychologists sent to help became carriers. There is no safe distance and no protective posture.  
- The Foundation's cure: terminate employees who reason poorly, freeze them in a shaft, and abolish its own ethics oversight for objecting.  
- Yoss's bomb metaphor — a thousand meaning-solvents packed together underground behind a fuse "that logically cannot be lit," guarded against an intelligence indifferent to logic.  
- The last image: a photograph of the sluice gate, taken from outside, origin unknown.

#### SCP-4489 — Apologies

**Object class:** Safe (Level 2/4489; Disruption Class Vlam, Risk Class Caution). Originally by Kain Pathos Crow; rewritten by djkaktus from SCP-089-ARC.

**Summary:** SCP-4489 is a small carved wooden statue of a cartoon hobo, held in a low-security item locker at Site-81; access requires the Site-81 research authority's permission. People in close proximity gradually begin to express guilt — usually over minor or entirely nonexistent slights they believe they've inflicted on those around them. With prolonged exposure the guilt inflates until the subject is inconsolable and impossible to interact with. Remove the statue and subjects return to baseline, on a timeline proportional to their exposure.

Addendum 4489.1 runs the escalation with clean comic structure. The control test — five minutes, one subject — produces a subject who apologizes for being such a boring test subject. Twenty minutes produces a woman weeping that she "didn't mean to breathe so much in there but it just couldn't be helped." One hour produces a man who has to be physically dragged out, is inconsolable for hours, and once calmed apologizes profusely for "having to pee so bad."

Then the last test puts two subjects in the room together for five minutes, and the article's real invention arrives: the guilt becomes a *feedback loop*. Eight seconds in, D-84544 apologizes for getting in D-91101's space — from more than three meters away, as a note dryly clarifies. D-91101 apologizes for making him feel that way. D-84544 apologizes for implying D-91101 didn't know what his own space was. D-91101 weeps at having put him in a position where he needed to apologize, and moves to the corner — which causes D-84544 to collapse in tears apologizing that he had to walk all that way "just to satisfy my unreasonable space needs." From there it accelerates into a shouting match of contrition — "OH GOD PLEASE NO WHY DO I DO THIS TO PEOPLE, I'M SO SORRY FOR INCONVENIENCING YOU LIKE THIS BY NOT PROPERLY ARTICULATING" — with each man apologizing for his own hideous face, his dysfunctional ears, and finally his entire existence: "My entire life exists only to be a mockery of a just existence." They end up screaming apologies into opposite corners of the chamber, facing the walls so as to spare each other their faces, and are removed after two more minutes.

**Themes:**

- Empathy as a runaway process — a virtue with no natural stopping point.  
- Guilt as recursive: apologizing for apologizing, forever.  
- Politeness escalation, and the horrible logic by which consideration becomes self-annihilation.  
- Comedy generated purely by an emotional mechanism rather than by an event.

**What makes it work:**

- The single-subject tests are calibrated as a ramp — boring, breathing, needing to pee — so the reader knows the shape of the joke before the two-subject test detonates it.  
- Adding a second person converts a static gag into a system, and the system generates its own escalation without the author having to invent new material.  
- The bracketed stage directions do the heavy lifting: "(Tears begin to form in the subject's eyes)," "(Crawling to the opposite corner of the room)," "(Through tears)," and the deadpan researcher note that they are three meters apart.  
- Typographic escalation — the transition into all-caps weeping — is used exactly once, and it's the funniest line on the page.  
- It's a rewrite that knows its job: take a weak archived concept and find the one interaction that makes it sing.

**The horror:**

- Almost none, and that's the point — but the mechanism is a real one: an object that can reduce a functioning adult to inconsolable self-loathing in under an hour with no memory of why.  
- The final line ("My entire life exists only to be a mockery of a just existence") is the joke tipping momentarily into something genuinely bleak.  
- The quiet implication of the containment procedures: it sits in a *low-security locker*, and its effect requires nothing but proximity.
