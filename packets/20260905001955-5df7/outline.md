## debt audit

The one purchase: stone shaped like an ear retains what is pressed into it and gives it back, as language, to a hand laid on the receiving face.

Derived from that alone:
- Cochlear east face, ridges worn by repetition: the shape is the function; wear is use.
- The world "speaking into it for four hundred years": people talk at walls that cannot answer; this one is shaped to invite it.
- Plaster inside, whitewashed each Advent: the monks maintain a seal without knowing it is one. Inside is the dead end of the spiral; nothing is delivered there.
- Monks cannot hear it: not because they are deaf but because they never touch the outer face. Novices enter by the flat west gate. No one inside has stood at the east wall since he was nineteen.
- Playback in his own inner voice: the wall delivers through the hand as language, and a deaf reader receives language in the voice he thinks in. Deafness is irrelevant to reception; it only means he has no competing channel.
- "Built to keep you out": the receiving face faces outward. Whoever is outside is who it faces. Every exile is that one.
- The wall turning his walking into words: footfall on the furrowed ground is pressure on the receiving face. Anything pressed in is retained.

Not derivable, cut:
1. "Every word was about him" and "the wall asked them to say his name." Four centuries of strangers do not speak of an unborn man; that is prophecy in a stone mask. Replace: the wall retains everything and an ear resolves what is addressed to its owner. Anselm hears the fraction that concerns him—his mother at the stones the year he entered, villagers who saw "the thin one" at the gate, and above all the eleven exiles he knew, each of whom stood here and pressed in what he knew of the men still inside. The thumbnail detail comes from a brother, not from providence. Cost: zero; it is the same purchase, sorted.
2. "How long before the wall needs to hear him die." A wall with appetite is a second impossibility. Replace: the wall has retained 410 exiles walking away and 410 of them stopping. "Until Ascension" is not hunger; it is precedent. No sent-out man has ever walked out of range in a lifetime, so the wall holds every death and can estimate his. The predecessor at the coast proves range, not desire. Cost: zero, and it is worse, because the deadline is arithmetic and arithmetic can be right.

## arithmetic

Unit: days from the sending. The house reckons in abbots and feasts; the reader reckons in days. Anselm walks; distance is stated in days' walk (12 miles a day in snow, 20 on dry road).

House: founded 411 years ago. One sending per Lent from the first year; Anselm is the 411th. Community now forty. To send one a year and hold forty, intake is roughly one deaf novice a year plus replacement of natural deaths; the catchment is the whole province's deaf sons, which is why the house is famous and why villagers come.

Anselm: born in Abbot Hugh's sixth year. Hugh was elected 37 years ago and is dead; Anselm is 31. Took the habit at 19, so 12 years inside, 12 sendings witnessed, 11 men he knew gone out before him. Sits third from the end at Vigils because seating is by profession date and only two are junior to him among those who remain.

Calendar: sending on Ash Wednesday, day 0. Easter is day 46. Ascension is Easter plus 39: day 85. Pentecost is day 95. The predecessor lived 95 days and reached the coast, 140 miles, about 12 days' walk; he then stayed. Anselm is told 85. The wall's figure is the median for a man of his build sent in a hard-winter year, corrected for his walking speed as it hears it. It has 410 data points. Decide: it is right to within a day. The story's engine is that the estimate is the one thing he plans against.

Exile mortality: shortest, 2 days (froze at the treeline). Longest, 3 years and 40 days (taken in by a hearing household; still heard). Median 80 days. Cause of death in order: exposure, starvation, violence to a mute vagrant, drowning.

What the wall holds about him: his mother's one visit, roughly 2,000 words. Villagers' remarks over 12 years at the gate, perhaps 1,500. The eleven exiles, each speaking or pressing in what they knew of him: about 25,000. Call it 30,000 words. At inner-voice speed, 150 a minute, 200 minutes. It finishes on the first day. After that it repeats, and adds his own walking.

Whitewash: 411 coats at a fifth of a millimetre is eight centimetres of plaster on the inside face. The seal is thicker than a hand.

Range: unbounded in practice. No one has tested the bound because no one has lived long enough to walk to it. At 12 miles a day, 85 days is 1,020 miles; the wall has never needed further.

## custody

The evidence is that the sending is a feeding and the house is an ear pointed at its own. It exists in two halves held by two archives that do not share a reader.

Inside half, held by the Chapter book and the charter: the founding rule that the house be built "in the figure of the ear, that the world may speak and we be not troubled"; the whitewash rubric; the lot by which the sent-out is chosen (the abbot exempt); and the list of 411 names with no date of death beside any. Alone this is pious metaphor and ordinary silence. Nothing in it says the shape works. Nobody inside has stood at the east face since the night he entered by the west gate at nineteen, told nothing, and the plaster has since put eight centimetres between every monk and the stone.

Outside half, held by the parish and the wall: the custom of telling things to the monks' wall; the villagers' belief that if you lay a hand on it you hear your own conscience (true—it gives back what was pressed in about you, and the hearing call that prayer); the row of unmarked graves for "the silent men," dug by the sexton at the parish's expense, roughly one a year. Alone this is folk piety and exposure. The village has never read the charter and does not know the graves are a list.

Why nobody connects them: the gate is one-way in both directions. To enter is to lose the outside forever; to be sent is to lose the inside. The only party who ever holds both halves is the exile, and his custody lasts 85 days, walking away, mute in the village tongue, literate only in Latin and sign. What he writes goes to the parish priest, who forwards ravings to the bishop, who files them with the visitation, which goes in writing to the abbot. The abbot reads it. The abbot cannot be sent. The abbot orders the Advent whitewash. That is the whole chain, one office per generation, and the office's answer to knowing is the seal. Chapter draws the lot. The abbot holds the letter. Neither hands the other its half.

And the exile's own testimony, the only full account, goes where all his walking goes: into the wall, the one archive that delivers only to the next man out.
