Father Okonkwo was the eleventh. He brought pistachios, which showed someone in the Office had finally read the file.

"You can put them there," Lauro said, not looking away from the television. Napoli were down a goal to Torino and the apartment smelled of shells and salt. "The others brought wine. One brought a rosary that belonged to Padre Pio. I sold it."

Okonkwo sat. He had the good sense not to take the chair facing the screen.

"I've been told to tell you," he said, "that she is deteriorating."

"She can't deteriorate. She's a reliquary. I built her from four hundred years of bone."

"She weeps. The tears are corrosive. They've had to move her twice."

Lauro cracked a shell with his thumbnail. On the screen a Torino defender went down clutching a shin that had not been touched, and Lauro made a small disgusted noise that was the most emotion Okonkwo would see from him all afternoon.

"I'll tell you what I told the tenth one, and the ninth," Lauro said. "I know what you want. You want me to kneel in front of her and say, *Bless me, Father, for I have sinned, I have made an idol out of children*, and then you want her to do something to a column of tanks near Van. I know the column. I've seen the photographs. You could get any of the Office's Jesuits to confess that. You could write it down and have a bishop read it."

"She doesn't answer them."

"No. She doesn't." Another shell. "Because they'd mean it."

Okonkwo waited. Napoli equalized off a corner and Lauro half rose from his chair and sat back down.

"The first time," Lauro said, "I confessed the thing I'd actually done. I walked her into the field and I said, *I stole the femur of a nine-year-old from a church in Lyon, and it was easy, and I would do it again.* And she looked at me, and she went out, and eleven hundred men just—stopped. Because I was sorry. Do you understand? I was sorry, and I would do it again, and she could tell both were true. That's what she eats. Not guilt. The gap."

He turned then, finally, and Okonkwo saw a face that was not cruel and not tired but simply finished, the way a solved equation is finished.

"I can't feel the gap anymore. There's nothing on the other side of it. You want her fired, you need a man who still believes he's damning himself." He gestured at the pistachios. "Eat. You came a long way. Tell them to send someone next month who's stolen something."
