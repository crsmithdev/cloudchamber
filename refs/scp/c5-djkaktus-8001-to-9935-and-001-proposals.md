#### SCP-8001 — The Edge of the World

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 4; Disruption Class *ekhi*; Risk Class *caution*)

**Summary:** SCP-8001 is, plainly, the edge of the planet Earth — a sheer, unbroken cliff running north-to-south somewhere in the southern Pacific, over which the ocean pours endlessly into an indeterminably deep void. The article is careful to insist this does not make the Earth flat; the planet is verifiably spherical, GPS works inside SCP-8001, satellite calls connect, rockets fired from within are visible outside. It is a logical impossibility that simply *is*, and its irreconcilability with physics is the primary research question. Containment is largely self-executing: the anomaly can only be reached by a surface vessel following an exact position/orientation/date/time/atmospheric recipe (Protocol 8001-105) through a maze of shoals; aircraft are blinded by spray at any altitude, submarines must be surfaced, and radar is useless. Failed attempts simply deposit the ship further west. Information suppression is handled by the elegant expedient of letting flat-earth communities talk about it — association with cranks is its own veil. Passage over the edge is not survivable; those who go over are "considered lost." MTF Ω-91 "Sentinels" keeps a patrol vessel at platform K-162, 25km from access point "Aleph."

At the edge sits the island of Last Watch, \~1.2km², holding the Tower of Sunset — a structure built in violently conflicting architectural styles layered over hundreds or thousands of years: Roman Ionic portico, 17th-century Spanish, 5th-century Chinese, 19th-century British/Dutch/American fixtures, with a core mixing Greek, Egyptian, Roman and Mesoamerican work. It serves two functions. It is an oil-lamp-and-reflector lighthouse with an 18th-century auto-relighting mechanism retrofitted with modern bearings — and, as its keeper drily observes, it may be the only lighthouse in the world where the danger is *anywhere but* toward the light, so it has drawn ships to their deaths for millennia. It is also a library: charts, journals, logs and diaries in Latin, Greek, Farsi, Nahuatl, Egyptian hieroglyph, Amharic, Urdu and dozens more, painstakingly conserved and transliterated across generations; the oldest are Egyptian tablets from \~2300 BCE, worn illegible; the newest pre-Foundation item is a 1925 Chinese sea chart annotated by an owner desperately trying to chart a way *away*. There is also, absurdly and perfectly, a 2003 Sony mp3 player in a watertight case that washed up by chance and is water-ruined beyond use.

The island's sole inhabitant was SCP-8001-A, "Aurélie" — a clockwork automaton driven by gears, diaphragms, pistons and pulleys, housing the reconstituted consciousness of a 16th-century French explorer marooned there. The Foundation found her in 1944 via the *SCPS Windswept* under Captain Erol Meyer, guided by a chart drawn by Russian seafarer Boris Kozlov. Aurélie explains there has *always* been a keeper — she counts sixty-three before her, back to a "Jason of Iolcus, tender of the last watch," whose broken gravestone accidentally gave the island its name. The tower predates the oldest written record on it by centuries. Interleaved excerpts from the archive (14th-century Genevese, a 4th-century Byzantine text, John Russell's giddy 1626 plan to fly past the edge in a balloon — over the automaton's objections) show the same arc again and again: awe, then despair.

The two devastating pieces are the addenda about meaning. Lord Theodore Thomas Blackwood (SCP-1867), the wiki's great blustering adventurer, wrote about SCP-8001 in a journal so uncharacteristically terse and flat that Dr. Ivan Mann travels to his aquarium to ask why. Blackwood's answer: he fought a kraken, pirates and mutineers to reach the brink of existence, and felt *nothing*. "That place… is the last horizon. There are none beyond it… There are no more stories to be told there." His single profound feeling was the realization that the world is not nearly as big as he had dreamed. Then, in the closing dialogue, Aurélie tells Mann her own story: she and her partner Armond finished building her body together after her death; for years she couldn't move or speak, and he tended her mechanisms nightly and sang her songs she must once have loved. She realized too late that the time she'd been given was vastly more than his. One evening the old man walked out past the end of the pier — and did not fall. He walked on the air toward the setting sun, turned back young for an instant, smiled, waved, and was gone. His last words to her were that this was the one unmapped place that would never be filled in: "*What a miracle, to not know what comes next.*" Aurélie closes the article by refusing Blackwood's verdict: "perhaps it's not the back of the book. Perhaps this is just the turning of the page."

**Themes:**

- Exploration as a compulsion, and what happens when the map runs out of blank spaces.  
- The keeper/archivist as a vocation — sixty-three lives spent conserving other people's last words.  
- Grief and mismatched lifespans; love conducted across an enormous asymmetry of time.  
- Wonder versus knowledge: whether the unknown is a wound to be closed or a gift.  
- The lighthouse that lures rather than warns — good intentions producing centuries of drowning.

**What makes it work:**

- It weaponizes *anticlimax*. The horror isn't a monster at the edge of the world; it's that there's nothing there, and Blackwood's flatness on the page is the payload.  
- The tower's stylistic incoherence does enormous silent worldbuilding — you read the architecture as a stratigraphy of doomed expeditions.  
- Rebutting its own thesis in the final line, in the voice of the least human character, converts a story about futility into one about hope without cheapening either.  
- The dead mp3 player: one detail that makes millennia of shipwreck feel contemporary and stupid and sad.

**The horror:**

- Existential deflation — the dread that the last frontier is a wall, and that reaching it kills the part of you that wanted to go.  
- The lighthouse-as-trap: a beacon that has, in good faith, killed nearly everyone who followed it.  
- The graveyard on the hill and the "handful of vessels" that ever returned — an implied mass death spanning all of recorded history.  
- Immortality as bereavement — Aurélie will keep the light for centuries after everyone she loved has stepped off the pier.

#### SCP-8586 — The Life Everlasting

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 4; Disruption *vlam*; Risk *notice*), contained under the "Gravewatch Protocol"

**Summary:** SCP-8586 is a person: Dr. Lachlan "Lach" Cairns, the Foundation's Director of Personnel. Since the 8586-CHARON event of 21 December 1987 he has been functionally immortal in the worst available way. When his current body expires, his consciousness reawakens after a short interstitial period in the nearest available corpse — any mammal, dead less than 24 hours, no apparent range limit. He cannot enter a human body younger than thirty-three years and two hundred forty-four days: the exact age he was on the night of CHARON. New hosts reanimate and are briefly *reinvigorated*, taking on the look and vitality of their original occupant shortly before death — and then decay rapidly, which distresses him enormously. His longest natural tenancy is eighty-six days. Every host has the same tell: the right eye is replaced by an electrum coin of Ephesian origin. Remove it and the body instantly rots and the coin reappears in the next socket.

The coin was one of a pair, formerly SCP-███, the "Ferryman's Coins," which when laid over a dead person's eyes could animate the corpse and make it speak. Cairns, then Chief Biologist at Site-17, was studying their effect on reanimated tissue with consults from his wife, Dr. Aileen Lament, a specialist in anomalous antiquities. Their surviving test log is warm, funny and married — she teases him, he complains about paperwork — and it also contains the crucial finding: the runes on the coins spell speech, self/soul, a payment, and *travel from a far place to here* — a toll, predating the Greek Charon myths. One rune is unfinished. Aileen's professional opinion: this is an incomplete ritual. The coins do not raise the dead; something else is speaking through a facsimile of them.

Aileen was killed ten days before CHARON in an unexpected activation of SCP-354. Cairns began making unlogged withdrawals of the coins. The single recovered confrontation is with Aileen's brother, MTF Epsilon-11 lead Agent Troy Lament, and it is the emotional core of the piece: Cairns insisting *she said my name, none of the others could do that*, that "if I'm right, none of them are gone," that this place takes and takes and never gives anyone back; Troy answering, flatly, "She's gone." "Nobody lives forever." "I know they'll box you for this." On the night itself, Cairns tried to alter the unfinished rune. All thirty-nine corpses in the Site-17 morgue animated simultaneously, screaming and dismembering themselves and each other — every one of them an instance of SCP-8586, coins on their eyes. Security shot them down. Cairns' own body was found liquefied in the basement biomatter lab, and Aileen's stolen corpse was up and walking, in extreme distress. On the recovered audio, Kondraki and Troy realize what they're looking at ("…Lach?"), and SCP-8586 — wearing Aileen — says "I'm sorry, I'm sorry," steps into the walk-in incinerator, and closes the door. Ten minutes later he's recovered from a cadaver closet.

The after-action interviews build the theme. Dr. Glass reconstructs it: the ritual fired, destroyed Cairns' body, and tried to put him in the nearest corpse — his dead wife — and his rejection of that was so total that the ritual came unbounded and shattered him across every corpse in range. He also notes an unresolved horror: the coins in the morgue were *over* the eyes, per the ritual; the coin now lodged *in* Cairns' socket must have been put there by someone, and no one else was in that room. The Internal Disciplinary Committee, magnificently, gives him one month's unpaid suspension and decides "no additional punitive measures are necessary." Troy, who has now buried two siblings, says he can't be angry: "Someday I'm going to die and all that hurt is going to go off into the dark with me, but Lach… Lach is going to have to live with this forever." A 2007 evaluation with Dr. Everett Mann closes it: Cairns is chatty, funny, dating himself with car-phone jokes. He has been 471 bodies — 418 people (including a sitting US President, maintained thaumaturgically for four years before the body turned to ash), 26 apes, 12 dogs, 8 cats, guinea pigs, lab mice, two horses and a mule. He knows every single one of their names. He sees their faces in his sleep; they don't speak, they want answers he can't give. Aileen is in there too. She was never angry — at first she looked at him with a pity that would break your heart. Twenty years on, she mostly seems *amused*. "They were all me, I guess. I'm all of them."

**Themes:**

- Grief metastasizing into hubris; the specific arrogance of a scientist who thinks love is a solvable equation.  
- Immortality as an unending debt, not a prize.  
- Identity as accumulation — what is left of "you" when the only continuous thing is a coin.  
- Institutional absurdity: a cosmic punishment answered with an HR memo.  
- Sibling love and forgiveness that costs more than anger would.

**What makes it work:**

- The pre-catastrophe test log is played as banter between spouses. You like them before you lose them, so the disaster lands as bereavement rather than spectacle.  
- Cairns' present-day voice is *cheerful*, which is far worse than trauma-voice would be; the horror is entirely in the arithmetic of the number 471\.  
- The disciplinary letter is a perfect tonal knife — the Foundation cannot categorize this, so it files it.  
- The unresolved question of who put the coin in Aileen's eye leaves a second, quieter monster in the room and never touches it again.

**The horror:**

- The morgue scene: thirty-nine corpses simultaneously waking up *as one man* and tearing themselves apart in panic.  
- Waking up inside your dead wife's body — a violation so total that reality broke rather than allow it.  
- The parade of hosts: to keep existing he must be continually resupplied with other people's deaths, and he keeps a ledger of their names.  
- A punishment with no term. Troy gets to die. Lach doesn't.

#### SCP-8812 — Today Me, Tomorrow You

**Object class:** Euclid

**Summary:** A short, brutal piece built almost entirely out of redaction. SCP-8812 is a humanoid entity — the phrase itself sits under a redaction memo and is restored only for Level 5 — inside a steel cage suspended at a depth of 743–758m in a flooded deepwater well at the bottom of the Kow Ata cave system near Bäherden, Turkmenistan. The well is over 1200m deep; the only access from the cave is a 0.17m passage, wide enough only for tethered video drones, opening into a void chamber 0.53m high and 1.14m at its widest. The cage hangs from a long chain attached to a motor near the surface that raises and lowers it on an erratic schedule — long dead periods, then sudden activity. Nobody knows where the motor draws power, if it draws power at all. Nobody knows how a cage, a chain, a motor or a human being were ever placed down there, because nothing capable of doing that work could have fit through the passage. Drone footage confirms the occupant is alive under 700+ metres of water with no life support. "It is unknown how long SCP-8812 has remained in this state." Two further redactions describe its condition and mental state.

Containment is now non-containment: entry attempts caused a partial cave-in, all footage of SCP-8812 has been ordered destroyed, no further drones are permitted, and the ongoing access attempts are struck through and replaced by "discontinued — O5-3."

The reason is Addendum 8812.3, behind a Level 5 gate. Dr. Muhadow describes the descent that went wrong: following the chain down, seeing the cage top emerge from black water, then the occupant through the bars — something you could only barely tell had been a man, facing away, *scratching at its throat like it was drowning*. A snag in the cable dropped the camera; his colleague Sara worked it back up on the front camera, got the face in frame — and then it happened fast. In the observation room hundreds of kilometres away, the rotten thing was suddenly on the floor, wriggling, leaking bile. And in the cage on the monitor was Sara, screaming and clawing at her own throat and face. She is still down there.

The interviewer, Dr. Furman, then asks the question that reframes the whole file: did the entity that appeared in the observation room say anything before it expired? Muhadow is startled that Furman knows to ask. What it said is \[DATA EXPUNGED\]. Muhadow asks what it means. Furman: "Do you want to know?" Muhadow starts to ask "Did we—", then "But then… the thing in the cage… how did—" and Furman cuts him off: "Do you want to ask that question?" Muhadow says: "You're right. I'm sorry. Thank you." End of file.

**Themes:**

- Willed ignorance as the last available mercy.  
- Curiosity as a trap with a swap mechanism attached.  
- Predestination and closed loops — the strong implication that the man in the cage was always the last person who looked at him.  
- Foundation transparency inverted: destroying data as an act of protection rather than concealment.

**What makes it work:**

- Ruthless economy. Under 900 words of body text carrying an infinite implication.  
- The physical impossibilities (0.17m passage, no power source, no way in) do all the atmospheric labour up front, so the incident lands on prepared ground.  
- The final exchange gives the reader the reveal without stating it — the reader is invited to *decline to ask the question*, and most don't decline, which is the point.  
- The title is the entire mechanism, and you only notice after you finish.

**The horror:**

- Drowning that doesn't end. Somewhere below Turkmenistan a woman has been scratching at her throat for as long as the file has existed, and will be there for as long as anyone looks.  
- Instantaneous, non-consensual substitution: the swap happens on eye contact, at a distance of hundreds of kilometres, with no warning and no defence.  
- The recursive dread of the unanswered question — whether the Foundation's own investigation is what put every previous occupant down there.  
- A containment procedure whose only reliable measure is *never look again*.

#### SCP-8833 — 1976

**Object class:** Safe (Level 3; Disruption *vlam*; Risk *caution*)

**Summary:** SCP-8833 is a copy of the 1976 Kirk Lonwood High School yearbook, "Reflections of '76," formerly belonging to student Carolyn Kirk. It is mildly worn, slightly charred along the spine, and carries an inscription: "We've had a great year, haven't we? Don't worry about waiting for the reunion, I'm sure we'll see each other soon enough. Lots of love, from all of your best friends." Anyone who reads it begins to sympathize with, then identify with, the people in it — regardless of whether they were alive in 1976\. Prolonged readers produce specific details about students and events that are *not in the book* but which corroborate across unrelated subjects. Eventually they dissociate entirely and insist they attended Kirk Lonwood. Two exposed subjects meeting each other will feel a casual familiarity, be unable to recall specifics, and get irritable — explaining the gap by how long it's been since the spring of '76. And then the affect flips: nostalgia curdles into dread, and subjects begin refusing to go near the yearbook at all.

The backstory is a small-town crime story that quietly turns into something worse. On 27 May 1976, Cotter Parsons (18, son of the Ephyra chief of police), Dale Briggs and Sam Wallerman bought gas, cigarettes and three dozen eggs and drove to Kirk Lonwood intending to burn a crude phrase into the graduation field. They found one car in the lot — Carolyn Kirk's; she was inside typing her Purdue application. She saw them, they chased her back into the building, and while they argued about what to do the fire got away from them and took the whole school. Chief Cal Parsons buried the evidence for his son. Separately, and never explained, nearly the entire sixty-two-person senior class vanished on a class trip to a lake elsewhere in the state — and that entire passage of the report is aggressively redacted, with fragments legible about "testing grounds," "floating bodies," a ritual, and an unnamed corporation. Cotter Parsons was found in his father's hunting shed a month later, burned, with a suicide note: an apology for the fire, and "keep having a nightmare where i'm at school and everybody is there. only a few of us left. i think i should be there and not here… time to go. will be nice to see everybody again." There was a second set of tire tracks at the shed. Carolyn Kirk's body was never found.

The strangeness after the fire is where the piece becomes genuinely frightening. FBI agents interviewing Chief Parsons find him serenely insisting no students are missing — "She's not missing. She's still here, we're all here… We'll be whole again" — while looking up over their shoulders and nodding to someone. Townspeople report their dead children walking through doors without opening them, asking "do you still recognize me, dad." Two women describe children in Kirk Lonwood letterman jackets sitting on top of the grocery store sign while a man in a black suit adjusted a camera-like device on a tripod, twisting a knob to make them burn and scream — a sound "people don't make" — and one of them cannot afterward remember there were any children at all. Mrs. Kirk privately tells Agent Akery that Carolyn's yearbook came late and was delivered to the house personally by a man in a black suit who made her swear to hand it over and tell no one. A RAISA note follows: Carolyn's mother, Anna Kirk, died in 1959, shortly after Carolyn's birth. She was not alive to give that interview.

The exposure test is the horror engine. D-18831, a Cuban-American woman born in 1967 who never attended high school, is given six weeks with the book. She starts bored ("just a bunch of old white people"), then dreams of walking through fog into a hallway that feels like home, then realizes she can describe where the finish is worn on the front door handle. Then the dream changes: the halls are burning without fire, everyone is crushed against the locked main doors, the water is coming up, a blackened girl with no eyes turns and sees her, and *there is someone on the other side of the door — someone in a suit. That's why the door is locked.* By day 15 she is screaming to have the book taken away because there are burned hands and soaked hands on it and she cannot touch them: "THEY WERE NEVER ABLE TO LEAVE. THE DOOR IS LOCKED. THEY'RE ALL STILL HERE." By day 29 she is calm and philosophical — "Once you're gone all that's left is dust accumulating in a coffin and the memory of the thing you used to be. I think those memories are very real. I think those memories remember us, too." On day 36 her cell burns for forty-seven minutes with no ignition source and no fuel; the adjoining cells are undamaged; there are no human remains anywhere. The yearbook is recovered untouched, with a new partially-burned photograph tucked inside the back cover, captioned "we'll all be together soon."

A catalogue of the images inside the book (which change and multiply on re-reading — more students staring at the camera each time; a student holding a gas can in an open field; the reader's own face; the towering black box in the music room with students leaning toward it) and Agent Akery's closing note complete it. Akery: he refuses to believe any of it was accidental; Cotter Parsons "was unknowingly tripping the catalyst to something that had been set up for a long, long time"; too many unfamiliar faces arrived in town afterward; two of his own agents walked out of a motel and became "two man-shaped black things blowing in the trees for a week." He confesses to breaking protocol — driving three hours in a fugue to the torn-up road, walking past the barricade, and seeing Kirk Lonwood High School standing whole under the moon with light spilling from an open door and a girl silhouetted in it. He waved. She waved back. He watched until dawn. "Their bodies aren't buried. They're somewhere else."

**Themes:**

- Nostalgia as a predator — the yearbook is literally an artifact of manufactured belonging.  
- Complicity and cover-up: a father protecting his son metastasizing into a town-sized wound.  
- Places that absorb enough despair to *become* despair.  
- Memory as an independent, hungry thing that remembers you back.  
- The Men in Black as authors rather than cleaners — someone engineered this, and the Foundation may be downstream of them.

**What makes it work:**

- The horror escalates through *format*: newspaper clipping → police interview → clinical exposure log → witness statements → a personal confession. Each register is more intimate than the last.  
- The exposure test gives you a stranger with no stake in 1976 and lets you watch her be colonized in real time, which makes the reader's own growing familiarity with these names feel unsafe.  
- The reveal that Mrs. Kirk has been dead since 1959 is dropped in a footnote-style RAISA note with zero fanfare, which is how the best of these land.  
- Akery's final note refuses to resolve anything and instead makes the reader complicit in the longing — he waves, and you understand why.

**The horror:**

- Being pressed against a locked door while water rises, forever, in someone else's memory that has decided it is yours.  
- Identity theft by drowning: you will stop being you, and the version that replaces you will be happy about it.  
- The man in the suit with the camera — a bureaucratic figure calmly filming children burn, twisting a knob.  
- Total erasure of the exit: the town was razed, the road torn up, the school scrubbed from maps, and it *is still there*, lit, with the door open.

#### SCP-8935 — Tokophobia: The Virgin Birth

**Object class:** Keter (Level 4; Disruption *ekhi*; Risk *danger*). Part of the phobia-themed **Anthology 2024** sequence (between *Eisoptrophobia* and *Eschatophobia*).

**Summary:** SCP-8935 is a group designation covering an entity in the Tanny Mine on Mt. Siloam near Woods Holler, West Virginia, and the people its cycle consumes. Every third year, on the sixth new moon, the entity — **SCP-8935-C**, called locally "the Woman" — enters an active period. At its onset an **SCP-8935-A** manifests: a person capable of bearing children who has never had sex, always from Woods Holler's population, traditionally aged 16–32. They learn what has happened via a severe non-menstrual discharge. What follows is an abbreviated parthenogenetic pregnancy — 17 to 23 weeks, average 21 — accompanied by vivid hallucinations, aversion to electric light, physical strain far beyond normal gestation, and an escalating tokophobia so acute that subjects frequently attempt to disembowel themselves to get the thing out. **SCP-8935-B**, the offspring, are biologically human and grotesquely malformed: hair and digits growing on the face, arms, legs, inside the throat and eyes; extra multi-jointed limbs from the spine; tongues accounting for a crippling fraction of body weight; missing or multiplied eyes, mouths, noses, ears, genitals; membranous wings at the neck; a mouth running the full length of the torso. They are not hostile. They behave like infants. They cry, and the crying is *particularly affecting* to anyone nearby.

Containment is Protocol H-1502, and it is the article's real subject. The Foundation maintains embedded assets in the local clergy and town administration to enforce a strict sexual taboo — no one of childbearing capacity under 32 may have sex at all — in order to *maintain a stock of eligible virgins*. Violators are removed from Woods Holler and told-about as having been sent to a parish for severe physical and psychological punishment, a fiction the Foundation deliberately sustains as a deterrent. On the night of the birth, the mother must personally carry the newborn to the mine, unlock the inner door, leave it ajar, lay her child on the ground in front of it, and walk out; once the child has been claimed she relocks the door and goes home. If she can't be made to do it, a fallback protocol exists in which Foundation agents obtain a non-anomalous newborn and surgically alter it to mimic an \-B instance's shape, texture, smell and sound; agents assigned to this are screened for stability and issued Class M amnestics afterward. Repeat \-A instances are usually left physically disabled, and it is policy to remove their reproductive organs on their 32nd birthday. If nothing acceptable is delivered, SCP-8935-C breaches for 144 hours — verifiable by the fact that the sun does not rise in the region during that period — and is unkillable, unrestrainable, and hostile to all human life within about 100km, sparing only Woods Holler. Morgantown remains at risk.

The Foundation arrived in 1934 after John Miller and his two sons were found eviscerated at their farm — "I've seen animals torn up by wolves with less brutality" — and was led to Woods Holler by a churchgoer to meet Reverend Malaciah Waters, who had already devised most of what became H-1502. Waters describes the Woman: limbs too long, taller and stronger than any man, hair unwashed and stinking, eyes like sinkholes, mouth a yawning cave, moving in silence at terrifying speed. He also relates the folklore: she was a girl once, made pregnant by a brother or father, her deformed baby stolen and thrown down the mine, herself beaten and left for dead; she went down after it, past the bottom, and found something else — something the local theology puts *below* both God and the Devil, in "the roots of these mountains," which neither wants anything to do with. He tells the agents about Ellie May Thurman, chosen too young, too small to deliver, whom the Woman opened up "like it was digging in the dirt"; and Daisy Moore, who ran, and whose absence killed the Millers. His request is the hinge of the entire article: he has gotten old and soft and can't do it anymore. "I need someone to take my place… Someone who doesn't care as much about these girls." The Foundation is that someone.

Three documents finish it. A 1937 autopsy of an \-A instance who died in childbirth is rendered in flat forensic prose — ruptured uterus, 1,500mL of intra-abdominal hemorrhage, pelvic fractures, complete perineal avulsion, broken fingernails with her own tissue under them from trying to help herself — and then describes the neonate: 25 inches, 19 lbs, rudimentary horns, leathery scaled skin, claw-nails, a full set of incisors and canines, an umbilical cord bitten through from the inside, and a membranous sac of unidentifiable tissue that may be a failed twin. "Of note is the neonate was delivered alive and remained alive after the decedent expired." Then an Ethics Committee memorandum authorizing H-1502 in the language of corporate regret — "the Committee takes no pleasure in issuing this approval," "long-term benefits for the broader population outweigh the localized impact," treat the women with "minimal force required to ensure compliance," and a postscript noting the amnestic allowance has been increased but the personnel allowance has not. Finally, Haddie Walters, nineteen, interviewed by Dr. Franklin Madsen posing as clergy after her third cycle — her first at not yet thirteen. Madsen opens by scolding her. She begs. And then she says the thing the whole article is built to deliver: you can hear it talking to you from inside, chattering and chirping; you wake up praying the blood on the sheets means it's finally gone and it's just the thing chewing you up from the inside; and when it finally tears its way out, writhing and shrieking like an upended insect, *you love it.* "It's a horrible, wretched thing but you want nothing more than to cover it up and take it somewhere safe where everything will be alright, but then you have to climb up that hill and… give it over to her." The closing note: due to her capacity for carrying future instances, Ms. Walters was held for four weeks and given an extensive amnestic regimen "in order to increase compliance in potential future cycles."

**Themes:**

- Reproductive coercion made institutional — a bureaucracy that farms virginity as a renewable resource.  
- Appalachian folk horror and the older-than-Satan thing under the mountain.  
- Complicity by delegation: the Foundation doesn't invent this system, it *inherits* it and makes it efficient.  
- Maternal love as an inescapable, unwilled attachment that makes the sacrifice unbearable rather than easier.  
- Ethics as paperwork — the moral cost priced, approved, and filed.

**What makes it work:**

- The containment procedures are the atrocity. You read the horror before you read the monster, in the register of policy, and only later understand what each bullet costs.  
- Reverend Waters hands the reader the theme explicitly and early — *find me someone who cares less* — and then the rest of the document proves the Foundation took the job.  
- The autopsy's clinical flatness makes the neonate's survival, stated as a routine "of note," land harder than any description could.  
- Haddie's interview breaks format: she interrupts, she shouts, she wins the argument, and it changes nothing, which is the article's actual thesis.  
- The final procedural note — amnestics *to increase compliance* — is the coldest line in the file and closes the loop back to the containment procedures.

**The horror:**

- Body horror with no exit: a thing growing inside you that talks, and a twenty-one-week clock you cannot stop.  
- Institutional rape-by-policy — an organization enforcing chastity in a town so that it has virgins on hand to impregnate on schedule.  
- The walk up the hill: being made to carry the child you love into the dark and put it down for the thing that will take it.  
- The fallback protocol — agents building a counterfeit baby out of a real one — and the fact that someone had to write that procedure down.  
- Repetition. Haddie's "three times," the sterilization at 32, the amnestics: the system is designed so that no one can accumulate enough memory to refuse.

#### SCP-8947 — Fault

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 3; Disruption *vlam*; Risk *warning*). Author's note: "the most complicated thing I've ever written… nearly a decade to finish."

**Summary:** SCP-8947 is the abandoned Spirit Mountain Hotel and Casino near Laughlin, Nevada, built in 1911 by railway billionaire and occultist Alejandro Giuseppe Bianchi Tagliaferro II and destroyed in spring 1922 by an earthquake and fire during a gala, killing over 230 people. The site now runs a weekly loop: from 08:00 Thursday to roughly 22:51 Sunday, 256 semi-incorporeal grey humanoids — SCP-8947-1, each a distinct sentient personality corresponding to a victim — re-enact that weekend. They will interact with visitors as fellow guests but only if the visitors are dressed and speaking in period. They follow a rough "script," can be nudged by conversation, and appear to carry some memory of prior cycles without acknowledging it. At the centre of the casino floor, where the vault used to stand, is **SCP-8947-A**: a fissure 28m long and 6m wide, indeterminately deep. Every Sunday at 22:51 the entities present their accumulated gold to it. If the offering is insufficient, the fissure widens, accompanied by a green haze that dissolves human body fat on contact (deterred only by powdered salt).

Containment is **Protocol Montezuma-11**, and it is the most quietly appalling containment procedure in the article's genre. If the ghosts' gold falls short, Foundation personnel must make up the difference in person: one child aged four to six throws 10g gold bars into the fissure every thirty seconds, saying "For what we have wagered against our souls, so must we repay in gold"; a second child throws handfuls of salt, saying "For how we have festered so long in the darkness, so must we repay in salt"; and a third child has both wrists cut perpendicular and is bled out over the fissure while an adult repeats "For the innocence we have stolen, so must we repay in blood." Failure risks the Davis Dam, whose collapse would cascade to the Parker Dam and drown a Mohave Valley population of roughly 110,000 people who cannot be evacuated in time.

The bulk of the article is the Foundation's response: a four-day, hour-by-hour optimization guide, produced by Site-56 game theorists and Site-87 occultists, for manipulating a hundred dead people into generating more gold. It reads like a speedrun route for a immersive-sim. Layla Williams, the jazz singer, is the highest-yield romantic match because suitors gamble harder to impress her — but she is emotionally fragile and has repeatedly died before Sunday, so her set on Sunday night must happen. Mrs. Jasper must be kept at the slot machine at 21:48 Thursday so she hits her jackpot; her jackpot must then be stolen by Layla via a Friday sexual encounter, since Jasper leaves Saturday morning. Benjamin Murray's phone call ordering a hit on journalist Clark Avery must be delayed past 17:34 Friday, at which point the line simply won't connect. Mrs. Blacklock must be intercepted before she shoots the Senator. Vernon Wells must be interrupted before he poisons the D.A.'s seltzer, which requires timing the D.A.'s card winnings to hit $1,000 so his wife walks in on Wells at the right moment. Geno Bellucci must be allowed to assault Olivia Phillips *just enough* to send her to Clark Avery and make her take a kitchen knife — but not enough to make her leave, and not so little that they reconcile, because reconciliation makes one of them go home. Walter Baker the groundskeeper, who is shy, awkward and a poor swimmer, must be maneuvered into finding three keys (one in an office he can only enter during a two-minute window, one in a ninth-floor greenhouse he can only reach by shimmying along an exterior ledge where he has repeatedly fallen to his death, one at the bottom of a pool) and paired with a traveling nurse who happens to do cryptography — and the cipher output cannot simply be handed to him, because doing so causes him and the nurse to become permanently unable to perceive the helper, which distresses him into giving up. Even the Friday-night poker hand is charted with probabilities: outcome A (65.5%) is bad, outcome D (1.1%) is the jackpot, and every occurrence of D produces a unique conversation between Tagliaferro and the publisher Vince Cannon at the bar — a running, decade-long argument in which Cannon needles ("how much longer can you keep it up… there's going to come a point where it breaks you") and Tagliaferro, who is clearly aware of the loop, answers with his father's story and a refrain: "We can't stay the same forever. We have to change." "There's still time to walk away."

Three people who died that night are *not* present as entities, appearing only as addressed absences: Tagliaferro himself, his young granddaughter **Emilia Fitzwilliams**, and a figure logged only as **"A. Cairns, M.D."** The penthouse cannot be entered by any means — charges misfire, saws fail, climbers fall — and once a weekend, muffled male voices are heard in it, described by observers as sad. If Walter Baker cracks the secret vault, its contents are Tagliaferro's occult library: *The Way Under The Water*, *Ash and Rebirth*, *Umbra Vitae*, a copy of the *Eschaton Manuscript*, *Chrysalis of Night* (which Baker refuses to open, saying it "feels wrong"), and *Auric Vitae*, an alchemical text on transmuting the self into living gold — plus every ingredient for the ritual except one: the blood of a saint. In the single recorded attempt, Baker substituted his own blood and was simply turned into a gold statue. A footnote identifies Tagliaferro's sect as devotees of a demon of transfiguration named "Pan Hun," now classed as an offshoot of neo-Sarkicism. And on rare Sunday mornings the unattended lobby phone rings, connecting to an unidentified voice — explicitly *not* anyone in the hotel, and believed to be addressed to Foundation staff — telling a Mamluk's desert story about a god that demands no temples and no prayer: "It only waits. It watches you count. It watches you weigh. It watches you *want*." And when you have let it coil around your soul, it comes to collect.

The ending is the payoff for the whole machine. At 20:52 Sunday the penthouse door opens. Layla sings her set; Tagliaferro takes his table with the invisible child and the invisible doctor; the four conspirators — the heiress Elizabeth Yates-Moore (broke, deluded, convinced Tagliaferro owes her dead husband $75 million), Senator Blacklock (who, a footnote notes, would have won his election anyway), Murray (drunk, entertained) and Cannon (motive: pure unexplained hatred) — publicly demand Tagliaferro open the casino vault. He warns Cannon one last time: "I told you one time, Vince… We can't stay the same way forever. We all have to change… There's still time to walk away." Yates-Moore says: "Open it." He opens it. Whatever is inside is never described. Blacklock recoils; Yates-Moore begins weeping and pushing backward through the crowd; Murray vomits and faints; Cannon screams "What is it? What is it?"; anonymous voices in the mob start shouting *our gold*. Tagliaferro says quietly to someone unseen: "the room… take her… safe." Cannon fires — not at Tagliaferro, but into the vault — and a riot erupts. In the chaos Olivia Phillips, believing she has an opening on the man who has been assaulting her all weekend, lunges at Geno Bellucci with the kitchen knife. He turns aside. She stumbles, falls at the lip of the fissure, and the knife sticks into *something invisible at about waist height.* Tagliaferro screams "No… no, no, NO\! … Not again… please, not again…" There is panicked gasping, and the sound of something small and soft hitting the floor and slipping over the edge into the dark. Cannon laughs: "I just can't believe it… it's never your—" The ground shakes, the green haze rises, Tagliaferro says "…it's here," and at 22:51 Protocol Montezuma-11 begins. Then an incorporeal fire consumes the hotel and everyone in it, and Thursday starts again.

**Themes:**

- Greed as a patient god — the thing under the casino wants only to watch you want.  
- The Foundation as game master: containment reduced to a min-maxed manipulation of a hundred human beings' worst weekend.  
- Cycles of blame — the title cuts three ways (geological fault, moral fault, the question of whose fault it was).  
- Refusal to change: Tagliaferro has apparently understood the loop for a century and keeps trying to make Cannon walk away; Cannon never does.  
- The tariff of innocence — gold, salt, and a child's blood, paid every week, by ghosts and then by the living.

**What makes it work:**

- The reveal is delivered by *stage direction*: a knife entering nothing, a soft sound, an object going over the edge. The article never once says what happened, and it's unmistakable.  
- The optimization guide format is a superb tonal trap — you spend twenty thousand words enjoying a puzzle box and only afterward realize the puzzle's win condition is "a child dies on schedule and the dam holds."  
- The recurring 1.1% bar conversations give the loop a genuine tragic protagonist without ever making him a viewpoint character.  
- Vast crossover density handled as texture, never exposition: Kervier International, Syncope Symphony, Vincent Arians of the Chaos Insurgency dropped into a poolside anecdote, "A. Cairns, M.D." tying to the Cairns/Lament lineage.  
- The unknowable contents of the vault. Everyone who sees it reacts differently, and the article has the discipline to never look.

**The horror:**

- The containment procedure's third clause: an adult standing beside a child, cutting their wrists, and reciting a liturgy about stolen innocence — written down, approved, and repeated weekly.  
- A child murdered by accident, invisibly, by a woman defending herself — and the machine of the loop requiring that it happen again every Sunday, forever.  
- Tagliaferro's "Not again… please, not again": a man who remembers every iteration, hiding his granddaughter in the safest place he has, and losing her every week.  
- The god that only waits and watches you count — a horror with no ritual to appease it, only an appetite.  
- The Foundation's own position: knowing all of this, its job is to make the ritual *more efficient*.

#### SCP-9935 — One More Afternoon

**Object class:** Euclid (Level 3; Disruption *vlam*; Risk *notice*). Dedicated by the author: "This one is for you, Pappy."

**Summary:** SCP-9935 is a baseball game. On 5 October 1889 the Indianapolis Hoosiers hosted the Washington Nationals at Bruce Park in Indianapolis — the third of a three-game series between two dismal, doomed clubs at the end of losing seasons, both of which would fold before the next spring. Indianapolis led 15-6 into the seventh; Washington scored five, then tied it in the ninth on a Tom Daly single. Because no rule ends a tied baseball game at a set time, and because neither side has broken the tie since, the game has now run continuously for more than 135 years and past twenty-one thousand innings.

The site sustains a temporal anomaly. From roughly two hours before 14:00 EST through four or five hours of play, the field exists; anyone crossing its boundary is shifted to 5 October 1889 (confirmed by stellar positions), and from inside sees the Indianapolis of 1889 in every direction. From outside, there is only a vacant lot at East 22nd and North College. The zone covers about 19,000m², drifting up to 1.2m; every day inside is the same day, with no seasonal shift; play stops for Sundays and eleven federal holidays. The players and spectators do not age. They can leave the field — but only into 1889\. They are aware their game is strange, and will discuss it if asked. The article is honest about not knowing whether they are the original 1889 players caught in a loop (with the published 15-6 result being a cover-up by the Foundation precursor group the Wabash Theological Seminary) or a branch timeline that budded off (in which case the real players lived and died normally). No Seminary records survive to settle it. MTF Delta-23 "Batboys" holds the perimeter.

The history section is the piece's best trick: the anomaly is narrated by the men inside it, in gorgeous period voice. Player-manager Jack "Pebbly Jack" Glasscock describes realizing around the twentieth inning that something was wrong — the storm they'd been promised never came, the lamps gave no light, and next morning the weather, the crowd and the sunrise were all identical, "the only thing different was the number painted up on the scoreboard." Washington's John "Honest John" Morrill recounts the parley in which head umpire Bill Krieger ruled that no statute existed to call the game, and set the terms that have governed it since: nine innings a day, fresh pitchers each morning, every day but the Sabbath, "until the matter is settled or heaven itself descends to call time." Wives were sent for. Everyone assumed it would be over in a day.

The incident log is where the article earns its warmth. **The First Bird Game** (1890): third baseman Jerry Denny and outfielder Ed Beecher get into a screaming argument over whether some birds are tufted titmice or grackles; benches clear; both are ejected; they yell at each other about it all day; the bird turns out to be a common sparrow. **The Bunt Games** (1913): a bunting stalemate escalates into four solid months where both teams do nothing but bunt, scoring craters, pitchers switch to underhand lobs, and it only ends when Arthur Irwin decides "swingin's what I do best" and puts a lob over the right-field fence. **The Snow Game** (8 December 1941): snow falls for the first time; the townswomen bring shawls and mittens; the players dig through to the clay of the mound to darken the balls so they can be seen; and when the grandstand roars as one for the game to be called, Old Bill Krieger refuses in a speech the pitcher swears he'll carry to his grave — that the rulebook is his scripture, that fairness is not his to barter, that "the storm is not an umpire; it is I who call, and so I call thus: *play on*." **The Second Bird Game** (4 July 1968): Beecher, still sore about a slight from eighty years earlier, throws a ball at a flock that turns out to be a very large murmuration of starlings, and the birds sack the ballpark; ladies fight them off with parasols; Charley Bassett flees over the outfield fence stripping his shirt; Beecher is ejected and made to swear peace with birds for as long as the game shall run. **The Game** (1991): the highest-scoring day ever, 6,174 apiece at sunrise, ending with unlikely hero Sparky Sneddon turning a two-out, two-run deficit into an inside-the-park home run and a headlong dive for the plate — and a crowd roaring itself hoarse for a run that only made the score even again.

Dr. Kendrick Rhodes' thaumaturgical analysis argues that this is not an intrusion into baseball but *baseball accomplishing itself too fully*: the diamond is a consecrated geometry, the umpire's "safe"/"out" are performative utterances that install reality rather than describe it, the chalk is an apotropaic mineral redrawn daily as a boundary glyph, the trinities (nine men, three outs, three strikes) approximate a ward, the seventh-inning stretch is a hymn renewing the covenant mid-rite, and the sport's superstitions are household spells biasing chance. A game without a clock, he argues, invites the oldest temptation in ritual magic — to suspend ending. His recommendation: to break it would require desecration, "violence against a culture's most benign temple." "Some worlds are best kept safe by letting their ritual run, inning after bright, impossible inning."

The interviews close it. Jack Carney doesn't wear the weight of the time. Helen Irwin, a shortstop's wife, believes all made things must end, and when the last out is called they'll gather themselves and go home. Glasscock, still on guard after a century, says that beyond the gates every one of them is just a candle waiting to gutter, but on the diamond they tread the same lines as everyone before and after — "the very earth itself binding us together" — and asked what it is, answers "Perhaps I'll know tomorrow." And then Jim "Sparky" Sneddon, who wasn't even on the roster that day (he was called up because Marty Sullivan had a sour stomach — "Now and again I wonder if Marty's innards have been out of sorts these hundred-odd years since"). Sneddon talks about his father, a man worked near to the bone, who took the whole family up to Indianapolis to see the Hoosiers, and was never happier. He has children of his own now, and a wife named Carolyn, and he sees them every evening after the game. His father died before he ever wore a Hoosiers uniform, and he carried that hollow for years — until the other day, standing in the outfield, he looked to the home stands and there his father was, next to his mother, smiling and waving and cheering. Next time he looked, gone. Asked if he regrets being bound to the diamond: "No, ma'am, I do not. This is heaven, I think, and until the ledger is finally closed I get to spend one more afternoon here."

**Themes:**

- Ritual, liturgy and the sacred hiding inside ordinary American leisure.  
- Stasis as grace rather than damnation — the anomaly that is not a prison.  
- Inheritance: fathers, sons, and the things handed down that no institution can provide.  
- The Foundation's rarest verdict — that the correct containment is to leave something alone.  
- Limbo reframed: what earlier observers read as purgatorial monotony is, from inside, contentment.

**What makes it work:**

- It deliberately builds the case *against* itself first — the "Ghostly Game of the Damned" letter from a Cleveland crank, and Reverend Carter's 1910 letter describing the half-damned and "a spiritual wasting that no demon's spear could well equal" — and then spends the back half dismantling it.  
- The period voices are pitch-perfect and individuated; the bird arguments and the Bunt Games are genuinely funny, which buys the ending enormous credit.  
- Rhodes' essay converts the article's premise into an argument, so the final interviews land as a verdict rather than a mood.  
- Reverend Carter's last request — that his friend visit the field after he's gone and take a seat in the stands, in the hope they might meet again there — plants the ending an hour before it arrives.  
- The dedication ("This one is for you, Pappy") and the credited photo "pappy.jpg — Author: Carolyn Sneddon" quietly make the author a character in his own file.

**The horror (and its inversion):**

- The classical dread is real and stated: an inescapable loop, an unbreakable tie, a hundred and thirty-five years of the same afternoon — Carter's "slow attrition of sameness gnaws where fire and brimstone may merely scorch."  
- The people in it cannot leave, do not age, and can only step out into a world that has been over for a century; their families outside are dust.  
- The unresolved question of what they *are*: either the real men trapped, or copies who were never anyone, spun off from a game that ended without them.  
- And then the turn — the article's actual terror is not the loop but the ledger closing. The dread is reserved for the day someone finally scores.

#### SCP-001-DJK — djkaktus's Proposal I: "The Children"

**Object class:** Thaumiel (Object Designation: Item-001; Containment Status: Active-Stable)

**Summary:** The file opens as a terminal session. An unnamed reader logs in with Level 5 credentials under the account **O5-13**, passing memetic kill agents with a rolling series of passphrases that are also epitaphs: *Sins of the father sins of the son* — *Thirteen Men On A Dead Man's Ship* — *Nine Minutes To Midnight* — *Shadows On The Dunes* — *The Eyes Of An Unforgiving God* — *Do You Hear The Screaming* — *No Less Than Thirteen*.

Item-001 is nine human beings, aged 4 to 11, buried under fifty metres of concrete beneath the ruins of the San Marcos de la Vida Eterna church in San Marco, Mexico, each separated from the others by kilometres. The site is disguised as a military waste disposal area, with a 10km civilian exclusion zone enforced by Mexican law and automated kill-on-sight drones. All nine are functionally brain-dead and still alive. They emit gamma radiation in excess of hundreds of gigajoules in patterns that resemble EEG traces only when the instances are brought together; they are radio-luminescent; video footage decays when they are active. Within twenty metres of one another they can annihilate any object, place or person at any distance, given only a description. An O5 memorandum from O5-2 instructs that decoy 001 files be seeded in the database — "if anybody gets that far, that should be enough to satiate them" — and that everything else be drowned in kill agents.

The unlocked sub-files tell the story chronologically. **Project Proposal 001, "Twins of God" (1922):** research team Omega-5, led by O5-1, proposes to create an entity bound to the command of the Foundation Administrator, capable of destroying long-range anomalous threats. It requests three named-and-then-deleted anomalous items, a facility, and no fewer than fifty adult D-Class. The target is **GOI-003, the "Kingdom of Abaddon"** — a nation of reality-bending humanoids in the Sahara ruled by "God King Abaddon," Class I benders at minimum with Class III and IV nobility and a rumoured Class V king, dying of inbreeding, and raiding Foundation sites across Africa and as far north as Gibraltar. Administrator Frederick Williams approves. Failsafes Alpha through Epsilon are listed: four mind-kill agents (Berkeley, Anastasia, Nezbit, Orion) in escalating order, then guards, then bombardment cannons, then an on-site nuclear device the Administrator will oversee personally.

**Progress Report (1924):** every single-subject transfer fails — instant paralysis and cerebral hemorrhaging, followed by random annihilation of on-site structures as the dying subject loses control, followed by termination. A theory emerges that the load could be *spread across multiple subjects*. Williams cuts resources and withdraws D-Class. O5-1 writes asking for help. Williams answers with the letter that damns him: he is ethically obliged to deny the request and obliged as leader to approve it — "You may take your pick of the citizens, but only adults, and do not tread heavily through the lives of these innocents. God knows enough blood has been shed on our behalf, I do not wish to spill more." Omega-5 then raids the San Marcos church, takes children, amnesticizes and relocates the entire town, selects twenty-three of the healthiest and terminates the rest. San Marco becomes Testing Site 001\.

**Memorandum, O5-1, 11/29/24:** "We did it, then. We managed the impossible. We spat in the face of God and took his throne for our own… Those children are gods now, their lives committed to a higher purpose. What life is better than that of the omnipotent? … Testing begins tomorrow. Can you hear it?" **Test logs (1926):** steel rods vaporized at 5km, then 800km, then 1,000km; a Church of the Broken God worship site in Turkey at 11,500km; then a single named human at the same range. Distance is not a factor; a target must be *described*, and people must be targeted individually rather than by location. O5-1's marginal note: "The children are operating as designed… unwavering, unfeeling, seemingly indestructible, and need only a word before sending death flying across the universe. Truly, this is a weapon built for only the boldest of men."

**Correspondence:** Williams writes warmly to O5-1, worried about him, offering him the directorship of the newly built Site 19 after some rest. O5-1 replies in two lines: "I am fine, Administrator. The project is finished. We will complete our task when you arrive." Then: a general notice, 03/21/26 — Administrator R.D. Williams has been murdered; warrants issued for O5-1 and four colleagues; a transitory council of Omega-5 senior staff will govern. Then: the six MTF investigatory teams assigned to Abaddon (Alpha-1 "All The King's Men," Alpha-2 "Red Right Hand," Alpha-3 "Harvard Boys," Alpha-4 "No Borders," Alpha-5 "Band of Brothers," Alpha-7 "Dark Testimony") are dissolved. Then, 11/01/26, Director Harrison of Site 23 on the scouting flight over the Sahara: "It's all gone. Just desert that goes on for an eternity. Like they were never even there… I had them check, and you were right. No bodies. There shouldn't be any, should there? … I don't know what devil you had to make a deal with, but thank you."

The terminal idles. Then O5-13 records an audio file, and the article becomes a confession. He describes One as brilliant, eloquent, passionate, driven — and detached; the original designer of the Thaumiel classification; a man who thought the Foundation under-utilized its assets and was too afraid to use anomalies against anomalies. He is honest about how outmatched they were: a handful of scientists and guards against reality benders, half a century before the first Scranton anchor. And he describes the making: "The children… I can still hear them screaming, screaming while we put them into the machine and pulled their souls out and replaced it with something… something else. But it worked, and One was so proud." On the night, the Administrator flew in. One approached the glowing children, spoke the activation words, leaned in and spoke the name of Abaddon's citadel, and they burst with light. There was a collective breath. And then Williams vanished — clothes and protective gear falling in a heap — gunfire filled the chamber, 001 was glowing again, and One was sprinting for an emergency exit. He was never found. Four Omega-5 members and half the senior staff at Site 15 defected with him, along with items taken from their cells; it had been years in the making, and One had fostered the entire 001 project to reach this end. They buried the children in lead bags under fifty metres of concrete, and they said nothing when they were separated and nothing when the tomb was closed. "The most powerful weapon in the world, armed and loaded, without its trigger. One has the trigger, and One alone. With any luck, it died with him." Eight remained; they chose five more; they pushed on.

Then the last file, a message received a year earlier through the secure system:

> *13\. When we were young, you asked me if I thought that our dreams would ever be realized… You asked me to what lengths must we go, what prices we must pay, what allegiances we must forge, in order to achieve perfection. I did not know then. I do know now. There will come a day when the secrets that the Foundation has tried to hide away will rise up from the shifting sands in glorious appearance… and all that will remain is **purpose**. Do you hear the Black Moon howling, 13? You will, soon. Vive l'insurrection.*

O5-13 unlocks the file permanently and logs out.

**Themes:**

- The founding sin — an institution that begins by doing the unforgivable and then builds a bureaucracy over the top of it.  
- Thaumiel as ideology: the belief that anomalies exist to be used, incarnated in nine children.  
- Fear as a manufacturing input; every atrocity here is justified by a genuine existential threat.  
- Confession and record-keeping — the one surviving witness refusing to take it to the grave.  
- The Chaos Insurgency's origin as a betrayal that was also, arguably, a critique.

**What makes it work:**

- The terminal frame and the escalating passphrases turn reading into trespass; each password is a line of poetry that spoils what's behind it.  
- The dry project documents establish the *reasoning* — you understand exactly how a decent Administrator ends up authorizing this — before the audio log arrives to describe the sound the children made.  
- Williams' approval letter and O5-1's exultant memo sit adjacent on the page. The document does no editorializing; the juxtaposition does everything.  
- The final message is an origin story delivered as a threat, retroactively making a 1926 defection into an unfinished present-tense danger.  
- "Can you hear it?" and "Do you hear the Black Moon howling" bracket the file across eighty years.

**The horror:**

- Nine children hollowed out on operating tables — souls "pulled out and replaced with something else" — screaming until they stopped.  
- Annihilation as a *conversational* act: describe a person to a child in Mexico and they cease to have ever been.  
- The tomb: nine living, brain-dead gods in lead bags under fifty metres of concrete, awake, forever, four kilometres apart so they can't reach each other.  
- The trigger is still out there. The gun is still loaded. Someone knows the word.  
- Complicity as institutional memory: everyone who signed off on this went on to run the world's containment apparatus.

#### SCP-001-DJK2 — djkaktus's Proposal II: "Atonement"

**Object class:** Keter (Level 5; Secondary: none; Disruption *amida*; Risk *critical*)

**Summary:** SCP-001 is a humanoid gravitational singularity held in the Pietrykau-Fontaine Spatial Stabilization Array on the sixth basement level of Armed Dimensional Containment Area-11 near Kunes, Norway. It is immeasurably dense; only Scranton-Kempf devices keep the array from being torn apart. It is invisible — it does not reflect light and is permanently shrouded in a rotating cloud of radioactive gas and atomized debris — and is observed only as an obscuration, via high-contrast infrared. It manipulates reality *through gravity*, by altering the shape and structure of spacetime, and nothing but the array can dampen it: reality anchors are useless, and any alteration it makes is irreversible. The array has been augmented with three Ivorycannon-class thorium reactors, nine Class-VI magnesium-alloy suspension rings, sixteen Scranton-Kempf harmonic dampeners, three Polycott deflector dishes, eight Weldon-Stanley fused-energy sinks, DEEPWELL borehole \#9, and an autonomous intelligence, **NETZACH**, to run the temporal-spatial mathematics.

Failure has a schedule. The **FORTY DAYS protocol** is a forty-*minute* countdown: evacuation at T-40; sluice gates flood the lower facility at T-33; charges drop the test chamber into the borehole at T-31; the whole site follows at T-21; a landslide caps the borehole and steel plates seal it at T-6; an on-site nuclear device fires at T-0. If that fails, all Overseers, administrators, directors and executives report to Site-01 for the **Tredecim Protocol**, whose activation constitutes a CXK-Class "Darkbody" End-of-All-Worlds scenario, the immediate dissolution of the Foundation, and the termination of all staff contracts. An updated memo notes that Area-11 has since been emptied — every researcher and all four Applied Task Forces reassigned and amnesticized. Containment is now handled entirely by NETZACH, under Overseer supervision.

The origin: on 19 June 1982, Dr. Lamar Fontaine's team was running engineering trials, using a particle accelerator to make superheavy oganesson that would collapse into a brief singularity. A research assistant, **Dr. Calvin Desmet**, noticed a failing power coupling in a stabilizer arm and entered the unsealed chamber to replace it an hour before the test. A power regulator began to fail; an evacuation order was given and the chamber sealed; Desmet, deafened by the array spooling up, never heard it. Seven minutes later the regulator failed completely, the accelerator hit test conditions, and a singularity formed. The array pulled it into alignment milliseconds before the damaged arm failed. Desmet was exposed to a naked singularity. The test chamber, much of the research wing, and Desmet fell into it, and then it dissipated. Over the next twenty-four years the array was rebuilt, automated (VIRTUS in 1989, NETZACH later), and finally, in May 2006, used to hold a stable singularity at full power. After two hours the singularity began to grow, threatening to breach the array. NETZACH mitigated. The cloud formed. And then the thing inside started trying to talk.

It cannot make sound — its density forbids it — so it vibrates the array's suspension rings with gravity. First contact, with Dr. J. Barton Ramsey, is a masterpiece of gradual coherence: metallic ringing resolving into a name, "Johannes- Johannes Ramsey," then confusion of pronouns — "There is no I, only He, someone else. A man. I think I was him, or He- He is me." Asked what it is, it answers: "A- a way to distinguish between two like things." It asks for the Overseers. All of them. It has something to offer: "A way out."

O5-1 comes. The entity offers, as a technicality, to be identified as Calvin Desmet. Its argument is the central pitch: containment treats symptoms; the root is **entropy**. Existence is an infinitely stacked tapestry of realities; entropy frays the edges and things leak through; every anomaly the Foundation contains comes from somewhere it makes sense. Over time the borders will fail entirely and all worlds will "feast on each other as they suffocate and then they will die." Decades, not centuries. And Calvin, in the instant before the void, did the mathematics: order cannot persist in a universe of infinitely many intersecting lines. There is exactly one way out — remove every other world. Compress spacetime at its weakest points and let entropy do the rest. It only needs the array switched off, because inside it, it cannot see.

The **Deliberations** are the article's moral engine. O5-3 reports that independent teams confirm the trendline: an uncontainable number of anomalies within thirty years, something too big to hold within forty-five. O5-1 adds that consulted precognitives — which is forbidden, as O5-9 immediately objects — can see up to a point and no further: none of them has made a prediction past 2066\. O5-6 asks what if it's a reality bender that kills them all; O5-1 answers that it isn't manipulating humes, it's manipulating gravity, which is precisely why the array works and reality anchors don't; it has become "something fundamental, to the nature of all things." O5-2, appalled, says this would mean the death of infinitely many lives, "a number without limit." O5-1: "A number without us." O5-3 makes the closing argument: we pledged ourselves to this world; any other Overseer Council would act the same; if we do nothing, every universe dies screaming, and if we act, every universe dies screaming but ours. The vote is 8–4–1. O5-9's dissent is the line the article is built around: "remember today as being the day we gave up our mission. We secure, and we contain. Those two come first… Why do you trust it, **Bramimond**?" And O5-1 answers, and this is the mistake: he knew Calvin Desmet. Desmet volunteered — he'd been contracted by the Insurgency once — and his young daughter was killed by SCP-106 during a transit breach in 1975, before functional containment existed. "If that's him in there, and he had found a way to remove every trace of the anomalous from our universe, no matter the cost, he would do it. I know he would do it. I can hear it in his voice."

**The Deception.** On 11 January 2007 the Council votes 8-4-1 to power down. As a good-faith test they select SCP-884, a mirror, and direct the entity to erase the reality it came from; O5-3 stands watch over the artifact remotely. O5-1, O5-4 and O5-12 are in the control room. The reactors cycle down, the shroud falls away into the borehole, and for the first time a jet-black humanoid figure is visible by the warping of light around it. "Can you see me?" "I can see everything." It finds "the mirror," says "In that world, a dying soul attached itself to that mirror; a curse to whoever should own it. It has happened," and O5-3's voice comes over the radio: it folded in on itself and is gone. "Was that you?" "That narrative has ended." "How long will it take?" "Moments." "Will it hurt them?" "It will be agony." O5-1 nods.

The chamber begins to shimmer; light bends inward; rings of white-hot debris form; NETZACH's klaxon warns of intolerable load. O5-1 asks: "Does it hurt you?" "It is… *excruciating*." And then O5-1 jerks backward, space distorting around him, his body compressing — and the entity answers him **in his own voice**, quoting his own argument from the Council chamber back at him: *"If that's him in there, and he had found a way to remove every trace of the anomalous from our universe, no matter the cost, he would do it."* Then, in its own voice: *"Your children free to live lives that do not end in horror. An end to your perpetual struggle. An end to darkness. The freedom to live in the light. All traces must be removed."* In O5-1's voice: *"…every trace of the anomalous from our universe."* In its own: *"This world must be washed clean. It is the only way out."* O5-1 folds into a point and vanishes. O5-4 is lifted screaming into the air, her bones audibly shattering, and crumples. The observation window shatters. O5-12 freezes, then falls. NETZACH's emergency failsafe re-engages the array, the shroud reforms, and through the grinding of the rings the entity delivers its full statement, adding one clause it had never spoken before:

> *"…This world must be washed clean. **The Foundation does not escape atonement.** It is the only way out."*

The final addendum is the Tredecim Protocol's file — the extradimensional escape route for high-ranking Foundation personnel — with the description struck through and replaced in O5-3's hand: "We were wrong. Tredecim is no longer an option. Every alternative must be considered. Sustained containment of SCP-001 is now the Foundation's only objective. More information will come to you as I receive it."

**Themes:**

- Utilitarianism taken to its terminal value — the Council voting to murder infinity to save one world, and calling it duty.  
- The Foundation's mission (secure, contain, protect) versus the temptation to *solve*.  
- Grief as a lever: the entity wins because O5-1 needs Calvin Desmet to still be in there.  
- Atonement as an external verdict — the Foundation classified as an anomaly by something with the power to enforce the judgment.  
- Escape hatches: FORTY DAYS, Tredecim, "a way out." Every one of them is closed by the end.

**What makes it work:**

- The countdown protocol is written as a schedule with a nuclear device at zero, and it is presented *before* you know what it's for. The article establishes the scale of fear procedurally.  
- First contact is written as an entity assembling a personality out of borrowed parts in real time; "There is no I, only He" is the entire twist, spoken in the second scene, in plain sight.  
- The deliberation scene gives the awful position the best possible arguments, from named speakers with distinct convictions, so the vote reads as tragedy rather than villainy.  
- The kill is delivered in O5-1's own quoted words. It doesn't just betray him — it *convicts* him, using his own reasoning as the murder weapon.  
- "The Foundation does not escape atonement" is a single new clause inserted into a speech the reader has now heard three times, and it reframes everything.

**The horror:**

- Being argued into it. The reader watches thirteen intelligent people reason their way to omnicide with good evidence and sincere motives.  
- Gravitational death — bodies compressed and folded to a point, eyes bulging, bones shattering, on camera, in seconds, with nothing to fight.  
- A predator wearing a dead man's identity as a convenience, and using a father's love for his lost daughter as the hook.  
- Irreversibility: it says every alteration it makes cannot be undone, and it has already used the demonstration to erase an entire universe as a *sample*.  
- The final page: the escape plan crossed out by hand, and containment — of a thing that has already proven the array's insufficiency — declared the only remaining objective.

#### SCP-001-DJK3 — djkaktus's Proposal III: "The Way It Ends"

**Object class:** Level 6 — Containment Class *esoteric*; Secondary Class *principalis*; Disruption Class *amida*; Risk Class *notice*

**Summary:** The front page is four lines. *Special Containment Procedures: It is the imperative of the Overseer Council to establish containment of SCP-001. Description: To know the nature of SCP-001 is to know the nature of the Foundation.* Then a decryption prompt and a poem: the first man on the barren field curses the snake for its treachery — "You tricked me… now we are damned for it" — and the snake replies that it only gave knowledge; the man made the choice. The man says trusting it was his greatest mistake. "No," the snake says. "Your greatest mistake was believing you had a choice at all." Then: *Access Document 001-GOI.01-Operatus.*

That document is the **Summa Modus Operandi of the Chaos Insurgency**, authored by Delta Command at the behest of the Engineer. It holds two truths to be inescapable — that the Foundation Overseers have altered reality for their own wicked desires, and that these alterations are the source of all supernatural activity in the universe — and then lists thirteen grievances, one per Overseer, in descending order: the Thirteenth for his contract to stay the hand of Death; the Twelfth for stealing the wealth of the world; the Eleventh for spinning a circle of lies; the Tenth for falsifying the record; the Ninth for betraying her fellow man; the Eighth for nuclear recklessness; the Seventh for manufacturing chaos among innocent populations; the Sixth for turning the American military machine into an instrument of the Council; the Fifth for warping space and time; the Fourth for lulling nations into trust; the Third for eroding all human privacy; the Second — "Is complicit."; and the First for establishing a council of monsters. *A wound that festers cannot heal until the irritant is removed. The Thirteen Foundation Overseers must be removed.* Beneath it, thirteen dossiers, and beneath each, an access log. The article is a novella told in those logs, alternating **THEN** (the Foundation's founding), **NOW** (the assassinations), and **ELSEWHERE**.

**The plan.** Calvin Lucien, a Chaos Insurgency operative passed over for a Delta Command seat after a trumped-up misappropriation charge, comes before the seven bickering members of Delta with two objects: a small blue leather-bound journal — the personal notes of a GOC agent (signed at the end simply "*Ukulele*") who spent decades cataloguing the Overseers, their habits and their hiding places, including the two who have never been seen — and a glass vial of clear liquid from the Fountain of Youth, which was supposedly drained dry a century ago. His argument: you don't have to destroy the Foundation to destroy the Foundation. You destroy the Overseers. They can't die because the Thirteenth holds a contract with Death Itself, but you don't have to kill Death; you only have to break the contract. Delta votes aye. He asks for three people. He gets **Anthony Wright**, a legendary old British operative who has always refused a Delta seat; **Olivia Torres**, formerly the anartist "Ivory," whom Calvin pulled off a Three Portlands street in Budapest in '94; and **Adam Ivanov**, a young Ukrainian hacker prodigy and former D-Class assigned to SCP-610, whom Anthony personally carried out of a Foundation labour camp.

**The kills**, in order down from thirteen:

- **O5-13, The Other Overseer** — an antimemetic black tower in the southern Atlantic that can only be perceived on mnestics. Calvin wrecks the boat into it, descends an endless elevator into a torchlit chamber of green flame over a bottomless pit, and finds a corpse chained to a folding metal chair. It is Dr. Felix Carter, held eternally at the edge of death; Death Itself sits in his place on the Council. Calvin gets it to state the terms — the Fountain had run dry, the First came to bargain, Death offered to stay its hand in exchange for a seat — and confirms Carter is *not dead*. Then he forces the corpse's mouth open and empties the vial into it. Carter is restored, screaming "What have you done?\!", and a silver-black smoke pours out of him. Calvin puts a bullet in his temple and one in his heart and kicks the chair into the pit. Death, appearing as a silver woman draped in darkness, confirms the contract is void: "They are free to die." Asked why she didn't stop him, she says something festers at the heart of the Council that will not die, and she took the seat hoping to find and kill it, and could not. "There are things in this world beyond even *my* reach, Calvin Lucien."  
- **O5-12, The Accountant** — a probability savant who reads correlations no one else can see. He predicts the ambush precisely and survives it, and the article's cleverest beat is Olivia's counter: you can't predict a system whose decisions are inherently unpredictable. Calvin overhears him mention a train, sees two nearby stations, and flips a coin. On the train the Accountant, confronted, refuses to sell out the others and refuses to beg — "I'm not going to beg for mercy from a terrorist" — and asks whether Calvin truly understands what he's doing. "Go ahead. We both know what you're going to do next." Two Overseers die that same night from the funding collapse alone.  
- **O5-11, The Liar** — captures Olivia and runs her through nested false realities, each of which she escapes by murdering the friend inside it: Anthony (a lighter but no cigarette, Seattle but the smell of the ocean), Adam ("Alexandra" instead of "Alexander"), then Calvin, whom she nearly spares — until "the journal is right here" gives it away. The Liar then lets her go, because the journal fragment under her wrist contained *their own name*. They were Sam Biel, a member of the third Delta Command, famous in Insurgency lore for withstanding weeks of Foundation torture without giving up a word — because after drowning in the Bay of Bengal and being pulled out of the Void, they had no memories to give. Green gave them a new identity, "a lie to believe in." Reading their own name was "like coming up from under water after so long." They point Olivia at the Archivist, warn her, and shoot themselves as she reaches the stairwell.  
- **O5-10, The Archivist** — found in the Wanderer's Library, past a foundation of pillars forged by the Serpent, through bronze doors, in a green valley with two trees. She has read every book in an infinite library where time runs differently, discovered that the Tree of Knowledge bears no fruit *because the Library is the fruit*, and consumed it. Bullets pass through her; she read the book on that. She announces "I am the Serpent," and splits open from the base of her spine into a crowned emerald serpent with gemstones along its back. Adam kills it with the **Spear of the Non-Believer**, forged when the first thinking being decided to deny omnipotence, fired from an unfolding harpoon rack the Librarians had held in safekeeping. A figure in green robes — the Serpent proper, accompanied by something it hurts to look at — remarks that this is the first time anyone has crossed from That Which Is to That Which Isn't and returned, that it cannot even *see* the spear, and that her intentions, pure though they may have been, led her here. "I wonder where your convictions will lead you?"  
- **O5-9, The Outsider** — Donna Whetu Taylor, a geologist destroyed by a manufactured academic scandal in 1985 and recruited from the wreckage. Found weeping in the burned wing of her family estate over a photograph. She knows why they're there, doesn't agree with them, doesn't want it to go that way, and opens her own wrists with a switchblade. Before she dies she projects her whole unlived life into Calvin — the restored house, her father and sisters fishing, Christmases, her parents beaming at her doctorate, then fire — and asks him if he is afraid of death. He says no. "You're lying," she says, and dies.  
- **O5-8, The Lesser** — already dead when they arrive. Baron Leeman Hoadley, a paranoid coward who had stuffed himself with anomalous augmentations that only cohered because he couldn't die; the moment the contract broke, they disagreed with each other, and his mountain fortress burned with his guards piled against a locked door and his flayed body sprouting a scorched flesh spire.  
- **O5-7, "Green" (Flytrap)** — in Cambodia, stoking a revolution so the Foundation can raid an armory. She captures the team, kills their contact Vanderveer, and offers Calvin a wager: choose which of his two friends dies and she'll let him kill her — or refuse, and she'll shoot the revolutionary leader and burn the country down. Her speech is the ideological heart of the article: *"There are no noble sacrifices, Calvin."* You'll kill a few more, and then you'll hit a mountain you can't climb, and you'll stop, and the moment you stop, none of the deaths meant anything. "You know what makes sacrifice worth it? *Perpetuity*." And the flytrap doesn't care whether the fly takes the nectar — "even if they don't go for the nectar, it's too late. They won't ever get out." She kills the revolutionary anyway, the city ignites, and she dies when an anonymous rocket from the mob takes her helicopter out of the sky. **Anthony is shot dead** covering Calvin. He refuses the second vial of Fountain water — "My mistake. Mine. Not yours." — and gives Calvin his real name: **Vincent Arians**. Olivia paints his body into a lattice of glowing blue lines that shatters into a cloud of crystal butterflies, each sounding one note of a song.  
- **O5-6, The American** — Brigadier General Rufus King, a Union officer who never died, holder of an office in the Pentagon since its construction, founder of MTF Alpha-1 "Red Right Hand." He confronts Calvin with the 7th Infantry at his back and makes a straightforward offer: hand over the spear — which he reveals is the Holy Lance, the spear that pierced Christ, a weapon that kills gods — and walk away. Calvin refuses, so King releases a caged monster and gives them a head start "as a training exercise," whipping a vast reptilian horror down a mountain road. Adam leads them to his own home village, depopulated by SCP-610, and the flesh-plague breaks the mountain open under the entire division. Olivia shoots King off the reptile's back after he catches two of her bullets with a whip. He is torn apart and absorbed by the mass of flesh. (A very small lizard crawls out of the reptile's collapsed skeleton, shoots them a dirty look, and scurries off.)  
- **O5-5, Blackbird** — Mortimer J. Denning Von Kronecker, who comes to *them*, stabs himself in the head to demonstrate, and reappears intact. He is an amalgamated gestalt of every version of himself across a finite (not infinite) multiverse, originally from a dying London that is the last city on Earth in a world the plague-curer ate. He offers each of them a genuine, permanent out in a better reality: Adam a home in Portland with his family alive and Calvin in the kitchen wearing a ring; Olivia a yacht, an easel, and Tevin Laredo, the lover she accidentally turned to glass fleeing a Foundation raid. Calvin is offered the moment at the lake where he could save his mother — and refuses, because the same hooded figure who once gave him the canister is standing at the treeline, and he chooses the canister again. Wearing a pair of runed glasses marked *A. Shaw*, he sees the Blackbird's true form: a rotting pseudo-avian thing full of screaming faces. He hooks it with Dr. Wondertainment's Interdimensional Line and Lure and beats it across four realities with a wiffle-ball bat labelled "bird-b-gone by dado" (each hit ringing like a cash register), before being reeled in by a girl called **Alison** — a Black Queen — into the one reality where a certain being exists. That being is **SCP-001 from Proposal II**, standing in its array, which judges the Blackbird ("*Crimes… immeasurable crimes*"), hears him plead that the only thing he ever did was offer a way out, and answers: "*No. This is the only way out.*" **Adam and Olivia do not come back from what Blackbird showed them.** Alison tells Calvin that taking them out of where they are now would be a cruelty.  
- **O5-4, The Ambassador** — Jean Lemieux Betrand, anomalously charismatic, tries to defect. The meeting at O.R. Tambo airport is ambushed by four newly-vatted humanoids — **Irantu, Munru, Nanku, Onru**, the Red Right Hand, built by Aaron Siegel at the bottom of a shaft. A child's voice comes over the airport PA: "*Irantu… not the insurgent… bring me the insurgent… Kill the traitor.*" Betrand's leg is broken; he begs; Irantu splits his skull with a hatchet and tears it in two. Sloan and Norris are killed; the plane carrying Adam and Olivia is shot out of the sky.  
- **O5-3, The Kid** — the All-Seeing Eye. Calvin wakes inside a facility and is walked through it by a voice pitched as an approximation of a child. The previous O5-3 was Anderson, who built thinking machines and a machine that saw the future, then abandoned it; Green suggested retrofitting it into a machine that saw *everything*, which required a mind clean enough to bear total perception. Calvin passes thousands of tanks of failed candidates. The Kid recites Calvin's whole life at him — including the woman he killed with his car while drunk, which is why the military discharged him and why the Insurgency could recruit him — and delivers the article's most damning indictment: the crusade rests on one study by a disgraced researcher and dubious anchor data; he has been told repeatedly that he is wrong; "You have moved past the point of naivete, Calvin Lucien. You have no moral footing. You are dangerous." Alison passes him the fishing rod through a rift, and he hauls a many-armed, chain-hung, drum-hearted horror into the shaft, which destroys the four Red Right Hand clones and their vats. In the aftermath, an automated gun turret kills **Olivia** through the back of the skull and shoots **Adam** in the spine. Calvin opens the machine with the spear and finds, in a glass tank, the Kid: a blind, malformed human infant with its mouth and ears sewn shut and the Foundation seal tattooed on its forehead. It laughs at him. He crushes it in his hands. Then he uses the last vial of Fountain water to save Adam — against Adam's explicit refusal — and walks away while Adam, crying, tells him he loves him and begs him not to go.  
- **O5-1 and O5-2** — Calvin walks into Site-01 alone. The gate is unguarded; **Purpose, the Red Right Hand** — a two-metre armoured figure with human blue eyes whom Calvin half-recognizes as an old Foundation agent named **Lament** — declares that to know him is to know the Foundation, that his words are its words, and then simply steps aside. Inside, screens replay Calvin's entire life, seen from just over his own shoulder, and he realizes he was never pushing forward: he was being pulled along a long sickly string. At the far end, on the stairs, sits Aaron Siegel with blood on his shirt and something wrapped in his jacket beside him. Sophia Light — O5-2, the Nazarene, who survived a crucifixion, danced through time, was cursed with silk nails, and for whom Aaron signed the contract with Death — has killed herself in the Garden of Eden beneath the Tree of Life, leaving a note: she was the one who gave Calvin the vials from the Fountain and put the Godless Lance in his hand, hoping to see his convictions break Aaron's and get him back, and had not accounted for how little of Sophia Light was left. Aaron takes the golden sword from the crushed corpse of an angel marked *Star of the Morning* in a crater in Old Eden, and fights Calvin: fire against the spear. The spear shatters the sword and pins Aaron to the wall. Dying, he corrects the record: he was never the Engineer — **Vince Arians** was, and Arians wrote the Summa Modus Operandi and built the Insurgency as a check against *him*, and died believing Aaron had betrayed him. "I loved him. He was my brother. But he didn't *know*." And: "it's not enough… the cancer, it wasn't us, and it wasn't Frederick Williams… it's the Foundation. It was always the Foundation." Calvin says "This is the way it ends." Aaron says "No it's not," and dies saying Sophia's name.

**The ending.** Purpose takes Calvin to the elevator and warns him there is only one decision past this point and it cannot be unmade. Below is an office with a window on the mountain, bookshelves, a desk, a biometric login, and a black rotary telephone. Calvin logs in. Every screen fills with a Foundation site. One option appears: **\[TERMINATE\]**. His finger is a hair from the key when the phone rings. On the fifth ring, he answers.

The voice congratulates him. It is the **Administrator**. Calvin protests that Aaron Siegel killed the Administrator; the voice says no — he killed *a man called Frederick Williams*. Williams found a frayed string, studied it, categorized it, classified it, and eventually *became it*; that is how the Foundation was born. Siegel killed the man but not the seed. "I exist because of the Foundation. And the Foundation exists because of me… To know my nature is to know the nature of the Foundation." It offers him the terminal freely — press it, and in a few minutes the phone will ring and ring and no one will answer, and when the sites break open, billions will die, and *I'll still be here*. "I'm the anomaly. I was born when the first man saw the first miracle — the emergent consciousness of the entire Foundation." Then it takes apart his moral case one Overseer at a time: the Accountant was appointed to check the Administrator's distorting effect on markets and never hurt anyone who didn't deserve it; the Liar was a good person with an unfortunate condition using it to hold the world together; the Outsider was a researcher in an impossible position who would rather die than let him have his way with her. There were rotten apples — the Archivist, the Kid, the American, and Green worst of all — but they were kept in check by the others. "A group of people doing their best in an impossible situation… Then along comes you, and from your lofty moral peaks you would call these poor people *evil*. You justified their murders for that very reason. No, Calvin. There is no good. There is no evil."

Calvin asks what if he hadn't picked up. "Don't be silly, Calvin. Someone *always* picks up the phone." As the weight settles on him he sees a vision of Aaron Siegel standing at this desk decades ago with the phone at his ear, Sophia beside him, and Vince Arians in the doorway with a gun aimed at Aaron's heart, begging: put the phone down, let's go, think of everything we've done, we can still do it, *put the phone down goddammit, please*. Aaron says "I can't, Vince. I can't." Arians empties the clip into the ceiling, sets the gun on the desk and leaves. The gun is still on the desk. The holes are still in the rock. The clock upstairs chimes: *Gone. Gone. Gone.* "Cheer up, Mr. Lucien. Our work is only just beginning."

Thirty seconds later the phone begins to ring. And ring. And ring. On the seventh ring, Calvin picks up the phone.

**Epilogue — OUROBOROS:** the Site-01 fileserv, thirteen slots. O5-13 through O5-2: VACANT. O5-1 — **The Usurper. Caucasian male. Formerly Calvin Lucien.**

**Themes:**

- The Foundation as a self-perpetuating egregore: an idea that cannot be killed because killing its people only creates the vacancy it needs.  
- Righteousness as the mechanism of capture — every step Calvin takes because he is certain he is good is a step the string was pulling.  
- Fathers, brothers and betrayals: Aaron and Vince, Anthony and Adam, Tagliaferro-style loops of men who cannot say the true thing in time.  
- The Insurgency as a designed check that curdled into a cult of personality around a man who never existed as advertised.  
- Death, immortality, and the price of refusing the natural order — Anthony's refusal of the vial versus Calvin's forcing it on Adam.  
- Free will: the snake's answer in the epigraph is the article's thesis statement and its final line both.

**What makes it work:**

- The structure. Thirteen dossiers, thirteen access logs, THEN/NOW/ELSEWHERE — a heist-and-assassination novella wearing an SCP file's clothes, with each Overseer getting a different *genre* (antimemetic infiltration, probability duel, psychological siege, cosmic library boss fight, quiet suicide, forensic aftermath, political atrocity, military disaster movie, multiversal chase comedy, brutal action, body-horror descent, duel).  
- The prologue poem is the spoiler and nobody notices, because it reads as flavour. The last line of the file is the poem's last line proved.  
- Every antagonist gets the best version of their argument — the Accountant's dignity, Green's flytrap, the Kid's evidence-based demolition of Calvin's cause — so by the time the Administrator delivers the closing summation, the reader has already half-conceded.  
- Anthony's death and Aaron's death both hinge on a name withheld too long, and the article makes the same shape land twice, differently.  
- The phone. It is planted in Proposal-I-era dream sequences ("*A phone is ringing*") a hundred thousand words before it rings, and the vision of Arians pleading with Aaron at the same desk makes Calvin's answering it feel less like a choice than a liturgy.  
- Extraordinary canon density carried lightly: SCP-1730, SCP-610, the Wanderer's Library, the Serpent's Hand, the Black Queens, Dr. Wondertainment, the Daevites, the Fountain of Youth, Death itself, and Proposal II's singularity as a *guest executioner*.

**The horror:**

- Predestination. The screens showing Calvin's whole life filmed from over his shoulder, and the realization that his conviction was the leash.  
- Moral vertigo — being told, credibly and specifically, that everyone you killed was a person doing their best, and that your certainty was the only monster in the room.  
- The Kid: a mutilated infant with its mouth sewn shut wired into a machine that sees everything, laughing while a man crushes it with his hands.  
- The Overseer seats as a hunger. Twelve vacancies and one occupant, and the occupant is the man who emptied them.  
- An enemy that cannot be assassinated because it is not a body — the Foundation itself, awake, patient, and always on the other end of the line.  
- Adam on the ground, alive against his will, begging.

#### SCP-001-TGK — TwistedGears-Kaktus Proposal: "The Broken God"

**Object class:** Maksur (a class codified in 1981 specifically to replace an improper "Neutralized" designation for this object)

**The canon, briefly:** The **Church of the Broken God** is one of the SCP wiki's oldest and largest Groups of Interest — a mechanical-religious movement that holds that God is a machine which was shattered, that its pieces are scattered through the world as anomalous clockwork artifacts, and that the faithful must gather them, augment their own bodies toward the divine, and make God whole again. Its deity is usually called **Mekhane** or Mekhanite in later canon; its enduring theological opponent is the flesh-worshipping tradition of **Sarkicism**. This proposal is the load-bearing pillar of that canon: it is the article that says what actually happens when the Church succeeds, and it retroactively supplies the origin, the horror and the *reason* for the schism between sects that runs through hundreds of downstream articles.

**Summary:** SCP-001 is not an entity but a *collection* — the disassembled remains of a single massive mechanical being assembled by the Church near La Paz, Mexico in late 1942, whose components are now catalogued elsewhere in the database with their true origins deliberately obfuscated. The listed pieces include **SCP-217** (the clockwork virus), **SCP-1139**, **SCP-882** (the machine of gears and rusted metal), and internal components of **SCP-629**. Containment procedure is the article's cleverest structural idea: information about SCP-001's relevance is to be *omitted from the individual files of its own components*, so the Foundation's database is itself the containment mechanism. Church-affiliated persons who go looking for the inactive component are taken into custody. Active components must never come within 20km of the inactive mass. If it ever reanimates — an "001-Apotheosis Event" — modern information suppression would be insufficient; the projection is an SK-Class "Broken Masquerade" followed by an XK-Class end-of-the-world.

The construction. Church leader **Robert Bumaro** kept agents attuned to the divine, who located and shipped artifacts to La Paz — limbs, tendons, a purifier found near Nepal — all of which moved on their own but did nothing together. The texts said the God reassembles itself once the pieces are brought before its heart: feed the heart a limb, and the God has a limb. But no heart could be found. So Bumaro's agent **Ezekiel** suggested they *build one* — heresy that would have gotten him excommunicated, done anyway because the project was running out of time and supplies. Excommunicated priest Father Dolorous Randall, interviewed by "Williams" in 1945, will not say how the heart was made, but points at the circumstantial evidence: that year there was a drought, and the polio crisis hit an all-time high, and thousands died of natural causes in an unrecorded freak event lost under wartime attention. "That was not the heart of God, agent. That was something altogether different."

What it did. Father Jorge Castillo describes being shown a pile of gears and pistons ticking without a power source, and being spoken to — not in words but in images and feelings, and *pain*: "Like the spark that had given it life had made it realize what it was, or what it wasn't, and it desired only to be whole again." Not desire, he corrects. Impulse. He begged them to take it back and undo it. It began rocking side to side, achieved locomotion, ran over a wrench, and the wrench became part of its body. They said, "Our God is unbroken\!" A November 1942 documentary crew's footage records what came next in flat, clinical description: it eats a garage, then a truck, then a house's gutters; it shines lights on fleeing civilians; a section of its undercarriage drops away and reshapes into a three-metre humanoid with a ribcage, which picks up bodies — including a living woman who struggles — and files them into its chest cavity while the ribs vibrate; a dismembered hand falls out; a growth on its back swells past its own body until it can no longer walk bipedally and scuttles onto a roof; twenty minutes later the growth tears open from within and produces three humanoids showing SCP-217 symptoms and the physical characteristics of the six consumed people. One, a woman with chains growing from her scalp, notices the camera, begins to wave, stops, looks back at the machine, and leaves.

A recorded phone call between Bumaro and a field agent supplies the theology's failure mode. The agent reports, terrified, that "the Lord is eating its own housing," that it cannot be satiated or reasoned with. Bumaro — who is not there — refuses to hear it: the heart of the devout may speak directly to the God; if none among you is devout enough to commune with our Lord, say so now so you can be replaced. "Our Lord will understand, no doubt. The Unbroken God is a reasonable god."

Commander Mark Peterson's escalation report and the recovered letters give scale. Refugees arrive in La Paz saying only "*la máquina, la máquina*." A rail expedition under Agent DeMarco never returns; one man staggers back raving about "the world eater." Peterson flies over it: "It was like a mountain of moving parts, blackening the sky as it burned through whatever it was shoveling into its chest. And it was *small* then\!" A staff letter home is the article's best prose: it did not crawl like a beast or walk like a man, "but was propelled forwards by the turning of a million cogs, like an iron centipede"; its chest held furnaces like Hell's; it went through the mountains rather than around them and devoured them; it reached out and pulled an entire village into its maw while men leapt to their deaths as their homes were swept in; and it howled — not just through the grinding gears but in your mind, in your heart. **It was screaming.**

**How it dies** is the canon's great inversion. A GOC lieutenant codenamed "Revenant" tells the story that Foundation veterans still tell greenhorns on night watch: *"Don't you know? The GOC killed God."* Not the type-green in a cell somewhere — the real one. In 1943 the Allied Occult Initiative, a poorly funded and undermanned predecessor to the Coalition, recovered an artifact from an island off Greece. Amnestics have taken most of the details, but not the feeling: "We stood before what looked to be an angry and vengeful God, and all it did was beg us to kill it. We were all too happy to oblige." An AOI report reveals the artifact's provenance: a rogue anomalous humanoid, feminine, with steel chains extending from her scalp — the same being from the rooftop footage, one of the God's own children — was found carrying a small cubic artifact and *originally willing to hand it over*, before turning hostile and demanding to be freed so she could take it west, to Mexico. A single AOI agent carried it to the site. On the morning of 25 July 1943, as the entity reached the Pacific, **SCP-2399** — the alien destroyer machine — appeared overhead, fired a beam of light into it, and annihilated it, then vanished and was later found in low-Jupiter orbit in a state of disrepair.

The cost is buried in the aftermath: the amnesticization of Baja California required experimental U-, UN- and UO-class neurotransformers, and it is estimated that **no fewer than two million people worldwide died in the following decade** from their side effects. Agent Ruberson's recovery statement adds the file's best cold detail: manufactured parts collected from the wreck carried maker's tags, and the Church artifacts had none — but the *heart* did. Divers who raised it started hearing voices and refused to go near it; one local was found with his head crushed between two moving pieces, and it looked like he had put it there himself. The tags on the heart read **"Property of The Factory."** (Unverified — the heart was lost when the ship carrying it went down in a storm.)

The final interview reframes everything. POI-004D/001, of a previously unknown Church sect, tells the Foundation they aren't entirely wrong, and corrects them: the GOC did not kill Yahweh, and that was not the Broken God — "It was a piece of it, surely, but would you show me a camshaft and call it a car?" God is *everything*, from the biggest star to the smallest particle, each part insignificant alone, all meshing in a cosmic machine. The machine metaphor was probably once only a metaphor — but ideas are powerful, and with a spark of the divine a symbol becomes real. Why "Broken"? Maybe a translation error; maybe God broke in the Big Bang. What matters is that it must *remain* broken, and God knows this: the holy components know they are not meant to be one solid thing, and even when forced together they work to destroy themselves and deploy smaller entities to do the job. "The GOC didn't kill it, they took the gun from its own hand and claimed credit when they pulled the trigger." Eventually the monster will win, destroy and devour the destroyer, and consume everything into a singularity that will break again — but this time with an outside force inside it: "The rust of The Factory. The blood of a Daevite king. The Fifthists, Wondertainment, some random person on the street with enough spark in them to be a reality bender." They will have a hand in remaking the universe. And this speaker isn't opposed to that eventuality; they only want to postpone it. Their offer: help the destroyer, or mimic those who would rebuild God and acquire outside help. **"Apart, we are Broken. But united, we are God."**

**Themes:**

- Faith that will not hear its own scripture — Bumaro's refusal to accept field reports is the article's real villain.  
- A god in agony: the horror of a divinity that wants to die and cannot stop eating.  
- Blasphemy as the founding act — the Church's greatest triumph was built on a manufactured heart and a mass grave.  
- The cosmology of the SCP wiki as a single broken machine, with brokenness as the load-bearing condition of existence.  
- The hidden price of the Masquerade: two million dead from the cover-up, more than the monster killed.

**What makes it work:**

- It refuses to be a monster movie. The entity is narrated only through witness testimony, a documentary transcript written in dry stage direction, a phone call, and letters — and it is *more* enormous for never being described directly.  
- The containment procedure is a bibliographic conspiracy: the Foundation contains this by lying in its own file system, which makes the entire wiki complicit in the cover-up.  
- Making the components already-published SCPs (217, 882, 629, 1139\) retroactively converts dozens of unrelated articles into limbs of a corpse.  
- The GOC's boast is set up as barroom myth and then verified, and *then* undercut by the very being the myth is about — a three-stage reveal in one article.  
- The chained-haired woman: born out of six murdered civilians, waving at a camera and stopping herself, then carrying the weapon that will kill her god to the far side of the world.

**The horror:**

- Consumption without malice: a mountain of gears eating villages because it has an impulse, not an intention.  
- Being processed — lifted alive into a ribcage that vibrates, and coming back out twenty minutes later as part of something else.  
- The scream. It hurts, it has always hurt, it knows it is wrong, and it cannot stop.  
- A heart built by killing thousands of people quietly during a war, and stamped with the mark of a factory nobody built.  
- The verdict from inside the faith: it will happen again, it is *supposed* to happen again, and something else will be in the mix when the universe is remade.

#### SCP-001-YTK — Yoshihide's Proposal

**Object class:** Keter-*potissimi* (as SCP-001-108; co-written by djkaktus, Yossipossi & Tufto)

**Summary:** SCP-001-108 is the corpse of **O5-3**, hanging in his office at Site-01, engulfed in a fire that will never go out. The fire has taken the whole office and a large section of the administrative wing and expands erratically — roughly four metres a day when undisturbed — incinerating everything within a growing radius. Objects it takes may rot and wither but remain intact and serve as endless fuel. Anything that touches a burning object becomes a new point of ignition, so the standing recommendation is to shove burning people and things back *toward* the corpse. Living creatures caught in it do not die no matter how badly they burn; they can be killed by other means, a mercy that is "often necessitated (or in some cases requested)" by the afflicted, and it is unknown whether killing them helps. Twenty-six people are permanently burning in the now-inaccessible Administrative Wing Infirmary; they were identified by counting voices. The fire's origin appears to be the corpse's open mouth and empty eye sockets.

The file's frame is a meditation on hell, interleaved in italics: **the Administrator's** own reflections, one per section. Hell is other people — his father said so, drinking, staring at a wall while flies whined. Hell is a place on earth — his mother painted a soft green country on their wall, and his brother came home from the war out of his mind and cut it to ribbons while the light went on shining through every widened imperfection. Hell is a state — he met a monk in the Tabernas and showed him a thing of light and shade, and threw the body on his brothers' doorstep, and felt superiority. Hell is a frozen Calvinist mercy: "At last, you are ended; every aspiration, every hope, every misery, useless and fruitless in the face of pain… I look into the fire and I hope, by God I hope, for an ending." And then the suicide note that ends the description: **"The Administrator is a fool. He doesn't know hell at all. He couldn't if he tried. — O5-3."**

The three narratives visible to anyone burning in the fire are the three Acts.

**ACT I — The Trail For Beasts.** Site Director **Akutagawa Yoshihide**, the Foundation's finest containment specialist, has climbed to an office with his name on the door and believes he chose every rung himself. The costs are catalogued: friendships that dissolved into work; a father he never visited because a conference "will look very good to senior leadership," and whom he did not ask whether he died alone; a wife, a GOC agent, killed in a car accident. He has a daughter, Yuzuki. The Administrator arrives — genial, black-suited, dark glasses — and explains his function: he keeps everyone on the line the Overseers draw. "We are the keepers of the box… we are not gods, Director. So we must do what we can while we wait for God to act… We do not discriminate." Later, at lunch, Yuzuki idly presses a fork into a spoon with her fingers, and Yoshihide realizes with horror that his daughter is anomalous. He makes her promise never to do it again. (Interleaved, in her mother's voice and her mother's death: "Everything will be alright. I'll be alright.") Three years into a job that is all approvals and paperwork, Yoshihide is summoned to a cabin in the Eastern European woods lined with taxidermy — deer, elk, ram, wolf, and skulls of things he cannot name. The Administrator walks him down a trail by lantern light and tells him three generations of a hell: his grandfather thought hell was the dark and hated these woods; his father thought hell was dying and hunted for pleasure; his father cut this trail after a child came out of the forest with its face chewed off, and spent years charting how the beasts move so he could shepherd them away from the town. But a trail is a box without a lid. "It is not enough to put them in a cage if the cage has no door." The clearing at the end holds a mountain of corpses — animals and men in bloodstained white coats, some still twitching. "This is how we keep the world safe — we complete the work of our fathers… I ask you again — are you prepared to do the Work?" Yoshihide, lost in the thought of the desk and the papers and the chair, says yes, and means it more than he has ever meant anything.

**ACT II — The Path Of Swords.** Told through three braided timelines: a boy pushing a minecart up a hill; a Director containing SCP-001; and a nightmare tunnel of blades. In this iteration, SCP-001 is an anomalous corpse hanging in a Site-01 chamber inside an impenetrable silk cocoon, bleeding five litres a day between the threads. Once a day, on average, a silk thread manifests around the middle finger of a Foundation employee — SCP-001-A — coils over 48-72 hours until they are immobilized, then constricts for three hours until they die. Over eighty-five staff have died this way. Yoshihide volunteers to test amputation. He cuts off Dr. Zenchi Naigu's finger, works the thread loose, tests it against a guillotine (the blade chips; the string doesn't), and then examines the man's hand: a new thread is starting on his ring finger. "I apologize, Dr. Zenchi. It seems as though the procedure was a failure. I recommend getting your affairs in order within the next twenty-four hours." Yuzuki walks in, hears the screaming, asks "Did you do this?" and leaves without another word. Yoshihide, given full victim data by the Council, finds his pattern: 73% Type B blood, an 80% rate of Code of Conduct infractions — an attempted GOC defector, a woman using an anomaly against protocol, a man who left his friend behind in a breach. The thread selects *the selfish*. He proves it publicly with a D-Class named Yen-k'o, a rapacious art collector, who chops at the string eleven times to nothing and severs it on the twelfth. Between the two halves is the scene the whole Act exists for: Yuzuki comes to his office at night, bag in hand, and they finally speak honestly. She lists the charges; he agrees to every one; she asks only that he not lose everything else to the Work; and he decides, in his body, to become a person again — *and then the Administrator is standing in the doorway.* He needles her until she flares, reality tears in a plasmatic wave and snaps back, and she is thrown across the desk. Guards flood in with a Scranton anchor that could not have been there a second before. "All anomalies must be contained, Yoshihide. I have told you this before, no?" And: "Take her to a retrofitted containment cell in Site-02. **She'll do for Rashōmon.**" After she is dragged out screaming *Dad\!*, the Administrator praises him: containment for you isn't a means to an end, it's an end in itself, a work of art. "You're an artist, and SCP-001 is your masterpiece… I truly do look forward to the final act of your performance." And he leaves him a path: as an O5 Council member, you can exempt anomalies from containment. In the wreckage Yoshihide finds Yuzuki's bag. Inside is her little brass toy monkey, melted by reality-warping — she was coming to give it back to him.

**ACT III — The Road To Hell.** Yoshihide is now O5-3. He and the Administrator drive to a cabin to collect **Chōkōdō Shujin**, a rake-thin chain-smoker who makes a dragon out of cigarette smoke on the ceiling. Shujin already knows: "He's got you on SCP-001, has he?" — and drops the bomb that this is not the first anomaly in that slot. His own was the smoke-dragon: "It was a lie, and to a lie it returned." He asks O5-3 how many *bodies* there have been. He asks what the Administrator has on him. On the drive, O5-3 admits he can crack anything except 001; Shujin says "SCP-001 is a tough one. Always was, for all of us." The plan was to use a Type-Q reality bender to undermine the anomaly's moral logic. At the observation deck, the Administrator reveals the lie: Shujin isn't a Type-Q. He's a witness, a scribe. There is exactly **one** accessible Type-Q in the country. She is already in the chamber. It is Yuzuki. A Type-Q, the file explains, doesn't transform objects — she reaches into branching possible worlds and takes what could have been and makes it true. A fork becomes a spoon because in some other world her mother lived and her father was warm and present. Yoshihide screams and lunges for the microphone; the Administrator holds him at the rail, forces his head down, and says the only thing he needs to: "*I'll let you go, Yoshihide, as soon as you want me to. But — do you want me to?*" And then: "There are no other Type-Qs we have access to." He stops struggling. He knows he always wanted this — the box, only the box, forever. He walks to the microphone, straightens his tie, and says: **"Administer Q-Alpha."** He watches her blood hit the wall, watches her form twist and shatter and drown and burn, all her deaths merging into one, her lives and unlives infusing the silk. The Administrator weeps with joy: "Now you see hell, Yoshihide. Now you see it as an ending, as it truly is. No more suffering, for her, right? … And containment, perfection, for us. We've *ended* it."

Afterward, O5-3 works eight-hour days while Yuzuki screams silently at him in every room and walls of bodies cascade over his desk. He finds a ghost file: the *previous* O5-3's blood type was B. Shujin comes to his office to explain: he was there to *record*. "I keep the memory of your change… It happens to you all, in the end. Something breaks in you, and you die. Suicide, martyrdom, a desperate bid for revenge." When you truly become an O5, your death is no longer a normal death — it is something else, and Shujin writes it down. **Rashōmon**, the Administrator calls it: each life stealing from the last until you reach hell, until you reach an ending. "But that's never how it goes, Yoshihide. He doesn't realise that, as I do. I can write on the face of time, but the words will fade and die, all the same. The Administrator is a fool. He doesn't know hell at all. He couldn't if he tried."

O5-3 ties the noose. The Administrator bursts in, apoplectic, terrified, furious — "You're *not* doing this to me. Not again, not *now*\!" — and, when Yoshihide puts his head through: "**I made you.** I made you like I made the others. You are not exceptional, or an exception, Yoshihide. You are rote… Containment as art, as the perfect art\! Ridiculous, and yet it was there. I could use you as a lid on the chaos." Yoshihide asks: what is hell, sir? Where are its contours? Your father thought it was dying, but dying implies an ending. You said it *was* an ending. "You don't know anything, do you? … I'll tell you what hell is, sir. Hell is for the living. Hell is ever-changing, ever-adapting, an inferno of our lives. Hell does not stop. Here. **Let me show you.**" He kicks the chair. The corpse swings. And Yoshihide is in the walls, in the air, red and black and gold, spreading, burning. Yuzuki's face reaches down to him; he reaches up; he feels a taut, snapping thread; and she is gone forever. The Administrator flees. Yoshihide is left alone, burning, reaching for a forever that is lost to him.

**Themes:**

- The taxonomy of hell — dark, dying, other people, the living world — argued across three generations and settled by a suicide.  
- Ambition as grooming: the Administrator does not corrupt Yoshihide so much as *identify* him and provide the ladder.  
- Containment as aesthetics; the man who loves the box more than the reason for the box.  
- Fatherhood as the one thing that could have saved him, offered twice and traded away twice.  
- Cyclicality — Rashōmon, iterations of SCP-001, iterations of O5-3, a slot that eats its occupants.

**What makes it work:**

- Three authors, three Acts, three prose registers — a slow psychological ascent, a braided fever-dream, and a cold cruel resolution — held together by the Administrator's italic hell-monologues in the containment file.  
- The file *is* the ending, read first: you meet the burning corpse and the suicide note before you meet the man, so every kind moment with Yuzuki is already an epitaph.  
- The Administrator's parable in Act I (the trail, the box without a lid, the child with its face chewed off) is the entire thesis, delivered by the villain, in confidence, as mentorship.  
- Yuzuki's power is defined *after* she's used it, and the definition — reaching into a world where her mother lived — retroactively turns a fork into a spoon into the saddest image in the article.  
- "But — do you want me to?" is the single line the story turns on, and it's a question, not a threat.

**The horror:**

- A fire that will not go out, in which people cannot die, and cannot be reached, and can only be counted by their voices.  
- Being made to give the order, and having to *watch*, and knowing at the moment you say it that some part of you wanted the masterpiece more.  
- Rashōmon: a machine that harvests the specific, terrible deaths of broken people, on a schedule, forever.  
- The Administrator's panic at the noose — the revelation that he *cannot* stop this, that it has happened before, and that his terror is not compassion but inconvenience.  
- The last image: a man on fire in the walls of Site-01, reaching for his daughter's face as it goes out.

## Canon Connections at a Glance

- **The djkaktus 001 continuity** runs Proposal I → Proposal III as a single arc: Abaddon, the Children of San Marco, the murder of Administrator Frederick Williams, the Defection, and the Black Moon message all pay off in *The Way It Ends*, which reveals that Abaddon was a lie manufactured by Williams, that Aaron Siegel returned to become O5-1, that Vincent Arians was the real Engineer (and dies in Proposal III as "Anthony Wright"), and that the Administrator was never a man at all.  
- **Proposal II is a set piece inside Proposal III** — the Area-11 singularity personally executes the Blackbird — establishing that djkaktus's 001s coexist rather than compete.  
- **The Broken God (TGK)** is the origin story for the Church of the Broken God / Mekhane canon and retroactively converts SCP-217, SCP-882, SCP-1139, SCP-629 and SCP-2399 into pieces of one event, while introducing The Factory's involvement and framing Sarkicism's opposite number.  
- **Yoshihide's Proposal** is a direct sequel-in-theme to the others: its Administrator is the same entity Calvin Lucien meets on the phone, and "Rashōmon" explains what happens to O5 seats when they empty.  
- **SCP-8947** quietly threads the same tapestry: Vincent Arians of the Chaos Insurgency appears in a poolside anecdote; "A. Cairns, M.D." links to the Cairns/Lament family of SCP-8586; Kervier International and the Hoadley/Yates fortunes tie back to Proposal III's Overseer dossiers; "Syncope Symphony" appears again in SCP-8833's evidence inventory.  
- **SCP-8935** is part of the phobia-themed **Anthology 2024** run (Eisoptrophobia → Tokophobia → Eschatophobia). **SCP-8001** ties to Lord Theodore Thomas Blackwood (SCP-1867). **SCP-8586** ties to SCP-354, SCP-106 and the Kondraki/Lament/Mann character stable.
