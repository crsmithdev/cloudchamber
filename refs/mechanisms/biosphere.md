# ANNEX — THE BIOSPHERE

*A catalogue of mechanisms, not of stories. Ten fields of ecology, evolution and epidemiology, each entry carrying the instrument that measures, licenses or administers the thing — an action level, a quarantine calendar, a studbook, a certificate, a listing rule, an inspection station — because the administering body and its instrument are facts about the mechanism and not decoration on it. Figures are given in the units the source uses; where a finding is contested, the contest is stated.*

*Entries state what the situation is and never how anything is produced. Items marked `[?]` are specifics a fact-check should confirm before they carry weight. Nothing here is ranked and nothing is recommended over anything else.*

## 1. Regime shift and hysteresis

- A regime shift is a system with two stable states and a threshold, in which removing the pressure that caused the transition does not restore the original condition; the mathematics of alternative stable states, catastrophic bifurcation and hysteresis has been standard ecological theory since the late 1970s and carries error bars, not metaphor.
- Critical slowing down — rising variance and rising lag-1 autocorrelation in a monitored variable as a system approaches a bifurcation — is the generic early-warning signal, and it is detectable in principle from a monitoring time series without any knowledge of the mechanism.
- Between 2013 and 2016 sea star wasting disease killed the sunflower sea star *Pycnopodia helianthoides* across its range — roughly six billion animals, about 95% of the global population, functionally extinct through most of California; the causative agent was identified only in 2025 as *Vibrio pectenicida* strain FHCF-3 (*Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2025).
- With the predator gone and a marine heatwave running concurrently, purple urchin densities increased on the order of 10,000% and 95–96% of the bull kelp canopy along roughly 350 miles of the north coast was lost (*Scientific Reports*, 2019), and a decade later it has not returned.
- Urchin barrens sit at roughly 120 urchins/m² while kelp recruitment requires densities nearer 2/m², so the two states are separated by nearly two orders of magnitude in the same measured variable.
- Starved purple urchins enter metabolic depression rather than dying, holding that state for years against lifespans of 20–40 years or more and resuming grazing when algae reappear (*Journal of Experimental Marine Biology and Ecology*, 2021), which decouples grazing pressure from grazer condition and makes the barren self-maintaining.
- The urchin's gonad atrophies to nothing in that state: the animal persists as a grazing test with no reproductive tissue and near-zero metabolism, indefinitely.
- Southern California's best-resourced clearance effort removed about six million urchins over a decade across 80 acres at a cost exceeding $4 million — roughly three years of volunteer diving and 150,000 urchins per acre.
- California's recreational red abalone fishery on the north coast has been closed since 2018, with reopening tied to stock and kelp indicators rather than to a date.
- The sunflower star captive-breeding programme — California Academy of Sciences, the Sunflower Star Laboratory at Moss Landing, The Nature Conservancy and CDFW's Shellfish Health Lab at Bodega Marine Laboratory — has raised juveniles from spawn, run caged ocean soaks in Monterey Bay in 2025, and drawn over $500,000 approved by the California Ocean Protection Council in September 2025 for reintroduction, with an eDNA assay as the monitoring instrument.
- *Pycnopodia helianthoides* was assessed as Critically Endangered by IUCN in 2020 and has been proposed for listing as threatened under the US Endangered Species Act `[?]`.
- The fourth global coral bleaching event, running from January 2023 into 2025, exposed about 84% of the world's reef area to bleaching-level heat stress — the largest and fastest-spreading on record, declared jointly by NOAA Coral Reef Watch and the International Coral Reef Initiative.
- Urchin barrens in Tasmania and along the Norwegian coast have persisted for four decades and longer, which is the empirical evidence that the alternative state is stable on management timescales rather than transient.
- Lake eutrophication is the textbook freshwater case: phosphorus loading flips a clear macrophyte-dominated lake to a turbid algal state, and reversing the loading to the level at which the flip occurred does not flip it back.
- Shifting baseline syndrome, named in 1995, is the observation that each generation of scientists adopts the state of a system at the start of its own career as the reference condition, so the target of any restoration walks with the workforce.
- Southern sea otter recolonisation of Elkhorn Slough reduced burrowing-crab-driven erosion of marsh edges by a measured margin (*Nature*, 2024), one of the best-documented cases of a predator restoring a physical process rather than a species list.

## 2. Marine chemistry and deoxygenation

- The ocean has lost about 5.0 Pmol of dissolved oxygen — 1.8% of the pre-industrial inventory — since 1770, and is committed to a total loss of 20.3 Pmol, or 7.4%, by 2650 even if emissions stopped instantly, so less than a quarter of the deoxygenation already caused has yet occurred (*Nature Communications*, 2021).
- About 80% of that committed loss lies below 2,000 m, where the ventilation timescale is centuries and no intervention has any purchase.
- Oxygen minimum zones are expanding in area and shoaling in depth, compressing the habitable water column for oxygen-demanding species toward the surface; the conventional hypoxia threshold for shelf fauna is 2 mg/L, and the Oregon inner shelf has recorded recurrent summer hypoxia since 2002.
- On the Namibian shelf the end state already exists: organic carbon accumulates faster than it oxidises, sulphate reduction runs in the sediment, and hydrogen sulphide leaves the seabed by diffusion, by bubbling, and by methane dislodging chunks of sediment that float up and leave craters.
- Water-column total sulphide there exceeds 100 µM, and the oxidised sulphur turns patches of sea milky turquoise at a scale visible from orbit (*Frontiers in Marine Science*, 2018; NASA Earth Observatory).
- Giant sulphur bacteria, *Thiomargarita namibiensis*, sit on that sediment at about 47 g/m² wet weight consuming the sulphide, and are the principal reason more of it does not reach the surface.
- Hydrogen sulphide at the relevant concentrations inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, so cellular respiration stops in an animal surrounded by oxygen; above a threshold concentration it also paralyses olfaction, so the smell that constitutes the warning stops before the exposure does.
- Surface ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1 units since the pre-industrial period, roughly a 30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration, and the aragonite saturation horizon has shoaled along the California Current.
- 53% of onshore pteropods sampled in the California Current showed measurable shell dissolution (2014), the shell dissolving from the outside in on a living animal.
- In 2007–08 corrosive upwelled water killed larval oysters at Pacific Northwest hatcheries and came close to ending the industry, which is the event that produced the monitoring apparatus now in place.
- The Burke-o-Lator, a real-time carbonate-chemistry instrument installed on hatchery intake pipes by NOAA and Oregon State, lets growers time their intake to the water; Hog Island Oyster Co. in Tomales Bay operates one, and the network is one of the clearest climate-adaptation successes on the coast.
- Seasonal upwelling in the California Current is the delivery mechanism for both signals at once — low oxygen and low pH arrive on the same water — and the Golden Gate is the tidal pump that carries it into San Francisco Bay.
- NOAA's Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory, the IOOS regional associations, the California Ocean Protection Council and the West Coast Ocean Acidification and Hypoxia Science Panel are the standing bodies; the Regional Water Quality Control Boards hold the permitting authority over land-based inputs.
- The northeast Pacific marine heatwave of 2013–2016, informally "the Blob", raised sea surface temperature anomalies over 2 °C across a basin-scale area and ran concurrently with the sea star and kelp collapse, so the two pressures are not separable in the record.
- Oceanic anoxic events in the geological record, and the euxinic conditions inferred for the end-Permian, are the only observations of the sulphidic state operating at whole-ocean scale, and they are the reason the Namibian shelf is treated as an analogue rather than a curiosity.

## 3. Toxins and neurotoxins

- Domoic acid is a neurotoxin produced by diatoms of the genus *Pseudo-nitzschia*, structurally a glutamate analogue and up to 100× more potent than glutamate at kainate receptors, concentrating in the hippocampus at CA1, CA3, CA4 and the dentate gyrus.
- The 1987 Prince Edward Island outbreak produced over 100 cases, 19 hospitalisations, three to four deaths and permanent anterograde amnesia in roughly a quarter of those affected, which is why the syndrome is named amnesic shellfish poisoning; the toxin was first found on this coast in Monterey Bay in 1991.
- California's regulatory action level is 20 ppm domoic acid in shellfish meat, enforced through a sentinel-mussel and phytoplankton monitoring network, an annual sport-mussel quarantine running 1 May to 31 October, and fishery-wide crab closures.
- Since domoic-acid monitoring began in 1991, no human illness from domoic acid in California-harvested shellfish has been reported (CDPH); by contrast the same programme's paralytic shellfish poisoning record runs to 582 illnesses and 39 deaths since 1903, which is the history the quarantine calendar was built out of.
- The action level protects against acute poisoning: a cohort study of tribal razor-clam harvesters found memory deficits associated with repeated exposure below the regulatory threshold, prompting a proposed tolerable daily intake of 0.003 mg/kg/day (*Marine Drugs*, 2025).
- California sea lions with chronic exposure develop hippocampal atrophy with ventricular enlargement, spatial-memory failure, behavioural inflexibility and spontaneous progressive epilepsy, and the toxin is detectable in amniotic fluid and milk, so a cohort is exposed before birth.
- The Marine Mammal Center in the Marin Headlands diagnosed the first marine-mammal domoic acid case in 1998 and reports to public-health officials, making a stranded animal a sentinel instrument in a human surveillance chain.
- A record *Pseudo-nitzschia* bloom along the West Coast in 2015 delayed the Dungeness crab season by months and triggered a federal fishery disaster declaration, the largest economic event the monitoring apparatus has produced.
- Saxitoxin and its congeners cause paralytic shellfish poisoning by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels; the US regulatory limit is 80 µg saxitoxin equivalents per 100 g of shellfish meat, harmonised through the National Shellfish Sanitation Program and the Interstate Shellfish Sanitation Conference.
- The other regulated marine biotoxin syndromes each carry their own limit and their own producing genus: okadaic acid and dinophysistoxins for diarrhetic poisoning, brevetoxins from *Karenia brevis* for neurotoxic poisoning and respiratory irritation in aerosol, azaspiracids, and ciguatoxins, which accumulate up reef food webs and are not removed by cooking.
- Tetrodotoxin, found in pufferfish and several unrelated lineages, is a sodium-channel blocker with no antidote and a management regime built entirely on species identification and licensed preparation.
- Freshwater cyanotoxins — microcystins, anatoxin-a, cylindrospermopsin — are monitored in California by OEHHA and the State Water Board against posted action levels for recreational contact, with recurrent advisories on the Klamath River, Clear Lake and Delta waterways, and dog deaths as the most frequent sentinel event.
- The hypothesis linking the cyanobacterial amino acid BMAA to the amyotrophic lateral sclerosis–parkinsonism–dementia complex historically observed on Guam remains unresolved after five decades and is treated in the literature as unconfirmed rather than refuted.
- Avian botulism, caused by type C toxin under warm, shallow, low-oxygen wetland conditions, kills waterbirds in the tens of thousands in outbreak years and is managed by water-level manipulation and carcass removal on refuges, which makes the intervention a hydrology schedule.
- The administering apparatus in California is CDPH's Environmental Management Branch (Marine Biotoxin Monitoring Program) for the quarantine order, CDFW and the Fish and Game Commission for season authority, county environmental health officers for posting, and NOAA/NCCOS and California Sea Grant for the bloom forecast products.

## 4. Fungal and wildlife disease

- There are on the order of millions of fungal species and only a few hundred that cause human disease, and the best explanation for the ratio is mammalian endothermy: core body temperature sits above the growth ceiling of the overwhelming majority of environmental fungi.
- The global-warming emergence hypothesis holds that *Candida auris* — which appeared as three phylogenetically distinct clades on three continents essentially simultaneously in 2011–12 and grows readily at 37 °C and 42 °C — is the first pathogen plausibly produced by that thermal margin narrowing (*mBio*, 2021); it is contested, with a 2024 review arguing climate is not yet demonstrably supercharging fungal pathogens, while the existence of the barrier itself is not contested.
- WHO published its first fungal priority pathogens list in 2022, ranking 19 species into critical, high and medium groups, and CDC maintains antifungal-resistance surveillance; *C. auris* is managed in healthcare settings as an environmental persistence problem, which makes the operative instrument a building's surfaces and its cleaning contract.
- Chytridiomycosis caused by *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* has caused declines in 501 amphibian species and the presumed extinction of 90, described as the greatest documented loss of biodiversity attributable to a disease (*Science*, 2019).
- Its congener *B. salamandrivorans* has not been detected in the wild in North America; USFWS listed 201 salamander species as injurious wildlife under the Lacey Act effective 12 January 2016, banning their importation and interstate transport, which is a border instrument deployed against a pathogen that has not arrived.
- White-nose syndrome, caused by *Pseudogymnoascus destructans*, grows on hibernating bats precisely because torpor drops body temperature into the fungus's range, and has spread to more than 40 US states and eight Canadian provinces since 2006, reaching Washington State in 2016.
- The interventions that work are instructive about the mechanism: a bacterial probiotic raised overwinter survival from 8.4% to 46.2% in Wisconsin trials, an oral raccoonpox-vectored vaccine showed no mortality or clinical disease at Idaho's Minnetonka Cave as of March 2024, and passive mine cooling and UV-C treatment are in use — the response to a thermal-margin failure being to make the room colder.
- Human fungal disease is now estimated at about 6.5 million invasive infections and about 3.75 million deaths a year (*Lancet Infectious Diseases*, 2024), revised up from prior estimates of 1.5–1.7 million, and the revision is a measurement change rather than an outbreak.
- Over 15 years, 24 translocations to 12 sites in Yosemite using mountain yellow-legged frogs drawn from populations that had evolved resistance produced reproduction at 9 of 12 reintroduced populations and new adult recruitment in about 79% of post-translocation survey years (*Nature Communications*, 2024), for a species that had lost over 90% of its range.
- *Phytophthora ramorum*, the sudden oak death pathogen, has been in California since the mid-1990s and was first identified in Marin County; it has killed millions of tanoak and coast live oak across 15 coastal counties, spreads by foliar infection of California bay laurel and rain splash, and is regulated through CDFA and USDA nursery quarantine, with the volunteer SOD Blitz survey and the California Oak Mortality Task Force as the mapping apparatus.
- Ophidiomycosis (snake fungal disease) caused by *Ophidiomyces ophidiicola* is now documented across eastern North America and in Europe, and is monitored by USGS's National Wildlife Health Center within the same surveillance system as WNS.
- Rabbit haemorrhagic disease virus serotype 2 was first detected in wild lagomorphs in the southwestern United States in 2020 and has since been confirmed in California, with a licensed vaccine and a reportable-disease framework administered through CDFA and CDFW `[?]`.
- Bighorn sheep pneumonia associated with *Mycoplasma ovipneumoniae* is managed almost entirely through spatial separation from domestic sheep and goats — a grazing-allotment and permit instrument rather than a veterinary one.
- Highly pathogenic avian influenza of clade 2.3.4.4b has moved through wild birds and marine mammals at scale, including mass mortality of southern elephant seal pups in Argentina in 2023 and detections in the Antarctic region from 2024, so the reservoir is now global and includes species with no prior influenza history `[?]`.
- The standing US wildlife-health apparatus is USFWS (which coordinates the national WNS response across roughly 150 partner organisations), the USGS National Wildlife Health Center in Madison, USDA APHIS Veterinary Services, the National Park Service, and state wildlife health laboratories including CDFW's.

## 5. Transmissible cancers and prions

- In at least three lineages a cancer has ceased to be a disease of an individual and become a transmissible clonal cell line with its own genome, passing between hosts as an infectious agent and outliving every animal it has occupied.
- Canine transmissible venereal tumour is a single cell lineage that left the body of one dog roughly 11,000 years ago and has been passing between dogs on every inhabited continent since; it is the oldest continuously propagating cell line known.
- Devil facial tumour disease appeared in Tasmanian devils as DFT1 in 1996 and as a second, independent transmissible cancer DFT2 in 2014 — two arising in one host species within twenty years, both transmitted by biting — and the species is now endangered.
- Bivalve transmissible neoplasia comprises contagious leukaemias of clams, mussels and cockles that travel through open seawater, with multiple independent lineages and at least one documented crossing between species (*Nature*, 2016), which makes the environment itself the vector.
- Pacific-coast prevalence figures for BTN run to about 10% in South Skagit Bay soft-shell clams in 2022, rising past 75% in Port Susan, and 5–10% in native basket cockles.
- The soft-shell clam *Mya arenaria*, the species in which BTN was first identified, entered the Pacific coast in San Francisco Bay in 1874 in shipments of Atlantic oysters begun in 1869, and was the dominant commercial clam in San Francisco Bay markets by the 1880s.
- Shellfish seed transfers between growing areas are licensed on the basis of laboratory disease-free certificates issued through CDFW's Shellfish Health Laboratory at Bodega Marine Laboratory and CDPH's shellfish sanitation programme, which is a functioning biosecurity system operating on a defined pathogen list.
- The Save the Tasmanian Devil Program maintains an insurance metapopulation under studbook management against genetic drift, and wild devils now show evolved resistance and reduced tumour virulence (*Nature Communications*, 2016), so the wild and captive populations are diverging under different selective regimes.
- Chronic wasting disease is a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy of deer, elk and moose reported in 37 US states plus Canada, Norway, Finland, Sweden and South Korea, and confirmed in California in 2024 in deer in Inyo and Madera counties.
- CWD prions are shed in saliva, urine and faeces by animals that appear healthy, and persist in soil and on plants, so a pasture stays infectious after every animal on it is dead and culling does not clear the ground.
- There is no confirmed human case; macaque transmission studies disagree with one another, with one finding no transmission to cynomolgus macaques (*Journal of Virology*, 2018), and CDC's standing advice is that hunters have animals tested before eating them.
- CDFW's Wildlife Health Laboratory runs hunter-submitted head collection and publishes positive detections as an open dataset, with carcass-transport restrictions, taxidermy rules and carcass-disposal contracting as the downstream instruments.
- Scrapie in sheep has been recognised since the eighteenth century and is the original transmissible spongiform encephalopathy; the UK BSE epidemic and the 1996 announcement of variant CJD produced the feed ban, the specified risk material rules and the surveillance architecture that all later prion policy inherits.
- British appendix surveys found abnormal prion protein at roughly 1 in 2,000 across the post-BSE cohort (*BMJ*, 2013; confirmed in *Acta Neuropathologica*, 2020) — a silent-carriage prevalence that has driven surgical-instrument policy and blood-donor deferral for two decades on the basis of an unknown number of people who will never be identified.
- Susceptibility and incubation in human prion disease are strongly modified by the methionine/valine polymorphism at codon 129 of PRNP, so the same exposure produces different intervals in different people as a matter of genotype.
- Prions were characterised at UCSF, where the Institute for Neurodegenerative Diseases remains the regional centre for the mechanism, and the National Prion Clinic and UK Health Security Agency hold the equivalent role in Britain.

## 6. Iatrogenic and long-latency disease

- From the 1960s to 1985, national programmes in the United States, the United Kingdom, France and elsewhere treated children with pituitary dwarfism using growth hormone extracted from cadaver pituitaries and pooled; approximately 7,700 US patients received it through the National Hormone and Pituitary Program, and it worked.
- 254 growth-hormone-associated iatrogenic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease cases were recorded worldwide as of January 2024, 36 of them among US NHPP recipients, and the longest documented latency in a US case is 48.3 years (CDC *Emerging Infectious Diseases*, June 2025); genetically determined incubation of up to 40 years is documented in the UK cohort (*Brain*, 2015).
- In 2024 eight recipients of cadaveric growth hormone were reported with early-onset Alzheimer's disease, five with dementia between the ages of 38 and 55, and archived batches were shown to contain measurable amyloid-β with seeding activity transmissible to mice (*Nature Medicine*, 2024) — a second, different seeded proteinopathy on the same route, surfacing four decades later; the authors state explicitly that there is no suggestion amyloid-β transmits in ordinary life.
- The lookback apparatus is a standing institution in its own right: recipient notification programmes, the UK CJD Incidents Panel, the National Prion Clinic, single-use and quarantine policy in hospital sterile services, and FDA blood-donor deferral criteria, whose product for most people it contacts is a letter.
- Human dura mater grafts distributed before the early 1990s transmitted CJD to well over 200 recipients worldwide, with latencies of years to decades, and corneal grafts account for a small further number — the same pooled-tissue mechanism through a different surgical route.
- Diethylstilbestrol was prescribed in pregnancy from 1938 until 1971, when a cluster of clear-cell adenocarcinoma of the vagina in daughters exposed in utero, presenting at ages 15 to 22, established the exposure; several million people in the US are estimated to have been exposed, and follow-up cohorts are still reporting outcomes in the third generation `[?]`.
- Thalidomide, marketed from 1957 and withdrawn in 1961, produced the regulatory settlement that governs drug approval today — the 1962 Kefauver-Harris amendments in the US — and is the reason efficacy and teratogenicity testing are statutory rather than customary.
- Hepatitis C transmitted by transfusion before donor screening became available in 1990–92 produced national lookback programmes and, in the UK, a statutory public inquiry; the latency from infection to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma runs 20 to 40 years, so the notification apparatus operates decades after the exposure ended.
- Radiation-induced thyroid cancer in people exposed as children after the 1986 Chernobyl accident is the clearest epidemiological signal of a long-latency iatrogenic-equivalent exposure, with excess incidence appearing about four years after exposure and continuing for decades.
- Asbestos-related mesothelioma has a latency of 20 to 50 years from first exposure, which places the diagnosis in a different regulatory era, a different employer and often a different country from the exposure, and is the reason compensation for it runs through trusts and statutory schemes rather than through ordinary liability.
- Human cells, tissues and cellular and tissue-based products are regulated in the US under 21 CFR Part 1271, which sets donor eligibility, screening and testing, tracking and adverse-reaction reporting; California's tissue and eye banks operate under CDPH licence, so the traceability of a graft is a licensing artefact.
- The structural feature shared by every entry in this section is that the product was already absorbed before the mechanism was legible, so the only available intervention is notification, and notification changes nothing about the exposure it reports.

## 7. Spillover ecology

- A 25-year reconstruction of Hendra virus spillover in subtropical Australia (1996–2020) found the causal chain runs through nutrition rather than through the virus: habitat loss removed the native winter-flowering trees that flying foxes depend on, roost numbers tripled after 2003, and the animals became permanently resident in agricultural landscapes (*Nature*, 2022).
- Forty spillover events occurred after 2002 where there had been none from 1996 to 2002, clustering in winters that followed climate-driven food shortage.
- Where a winter nectar pulse in remnant native forest drew aggregations of more than 100,000 bats away from farmland, spillover probability fell from 0.014 to effectively zero — making ecological restoration a measured pandemic-prevention intervention that works by moving the reservoir away from people.
- Hendra virus has infected only a handful of people, of whom the majority died, and kills most infected horses; the epidemiological significance is the reservoir dynamic rather than the case count `[?]`.
- Nipah virus produces near-annual outbreaks in Bangladesh and India with case fatality frequently above 70%, and the seasonality of exposure is tied to a specific harvesting practice, which makes the primary control instrument a physical barrier and a seasonal advisory rather than a medical countermeasure.
- Land-use change is the dominant global driver: sites under substantial human use hold larger populations and a greater proportion of species that host human-shared pathogens than nearby undisturbed sites (*Nature*, 2020), so zoonotic host communities are assembled by land conversion.
- Highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 was first confirmed in US dairy cattle on 25 March 2024 and has since been confirmed in herds in 19 states; 70 human H5 infections have been confirmed in the United States since the outbreak began, 41 of them in dairy farm workers, and the culling and mortality rate in cattle has averaged 2% or less.
- USDA's April 2024 federal order requires testing of lactating dairy cattle for H5N1 before interstate movement and reporting of positives to APHIS; a second federal order in December 2024 established the National Milk Testing Strategy, sampling raw milk from silos at processing plants, with 45 states participating, and two genotypes — B3.13 and D1.1 — have been distinguished in the cattle system.
- California's mosquito and vector control districts are independent special districts with their own tax revenue, elected or appointed boards, and statutory powers of entry and abatement under the Mosquito Abatement and Vector Control District Law, which makes vector control a unit of local government rather than a health-department function.
- West Nile virus has been established in California since 2003 and is tracked through dead-bird reporting, sentinel chicken flocks and mosquito pool testing, with the public dead-bird hotline functioning as a distributed detection instrument.
- Lyme disease risk in California is depressed relative to the Northeast in part because the western fence lizard, a principal host for juvenile *Ixodes pacificus*, carries a borreliacidal factor in its blood, so a common backyard reptile is a component of the regional risk model.
- Hantavirus (Sin Nombre virus) incidence tracks deer mouse population dynamics, which track precipitation and seed masting with a lag, so exposure risk in a given year is forecastable from the previous year's weather.
- Plague is enzootic in California ground squirrels and other rodents across the Sierra and Coast ranges, and CDPH's Vector-Borne Disease Section conducts routine flea and rodent surveillance, with campground closures as the standard control action.
- *Toxoplasma gondii* has felids as its only definitive hosts, and oocysts entering coastal waters through runoff have been implicated in southern sea otter mortality including fatal steatitis (*Frontiers in Marine Science*, 2023), which makes storm-drain jurisdiction and household waste disposal a wildlife-mortality pathway administered by municipal utilities.
- CDFW's Marine Wildlife Veterinary Care and Research Center in Santa Cruz performs the necropsies that establish those causes of death, so the sea otter functions as a monitored indicator of terrestrial runoff.
- The rodent literature on *Toxoplasma*-associated behavioural change is well replicated and a parasite-produced dopamine mechanism has been published (*Nature Communications*, 2025); the corresponding human behavioural literature is not supported by the strongest test of it, a population-representative birth cohort finding essentially null associations across brain and behaviour measures (*PLOS One*, 2016).

## 8. Genetics, gene flow and mobile elements

- Roughly 8% of the human genome is derived from endogenous retroviruses, and at least one such sequence, the HERV-W envelope gene syncytin-1, has been captured for a functional role in placental development, so a substantial fraction of the genome is a record of past infection put to work.
- Plasmids, integrons, insertion sequences and transposons move genetic material between unrelated bacteria including across genera, which is why resistance traits appear in species that never received the selecting agent.
- Antimicrobial resistance was directly attributable to 1.14 million deaths in 2021 and is forecast at 1.91 million deaths a year by 2050 with 39 million cumulative deaths between 2025 and 2050 (*The Lancet*, 2024); it is a rate rather than a discrete event, and its administering instruments are stewardship programmes, WHO's bacterial priority pathogens list (revised 2024), and surveillance systems such as NARMS and GLASS.
- Argentine ants (*Linepithema humile*) in their native range live in ordinary mutually hostile colonies, but the introduced California population forms a single supercolony extending from San Diego to Ukiah in which ants from either end recognise each other as nestmates and do not fight.
- The recognition system is chemical — methyl-branched cuticular hydrocarbons read on contact — and the introduced population passed through a genetic bottleneck that flattened the diversity of those cues, so the loss of the capacity to tell one another apart is what removed intraspecific territorial conflict and made the colony continental; unicolonial populations in Europe and Japan have been argued to belong to the same globally distributed entity (2009).
- Gut and oral microbiome strains are transmitted between people at measurable rates — about 50% strain sharing mother to infant in the first year, 12% between cohabiting adults for gut strains and 32% for oral strains, with strains persisting six months — and the authors explicitly raise transmissibility as a consideration for microbiome-associated non-infectious disease (*Nature*, 2023).
- There has been no gene-drive field release anywhere: releases by Target Malaria in Burkina Faso in 2019 and August 2025 were of non-drive lines, and in August 2025 the national authorities suspended the programme's research and ordered the modified lines destroyed, citing transparency, divided expert assessment and national biosafety capacity.
- The governing instruments for that class of work are the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety and national biosafety committees, with CBD Conference of the Parties decisions on synthetic biology and engineered gene drives setting the international expectations, including case-by-case risk assessment and free, prior and informed consent of potentially affected communities.
- The Nagoya Protocol governs access to genetic resources and benefit-sharing, and the unresolved treatment of digital sequence information under it means that a sequence in a public database may carry obligations that the physical sample it came from also carries.
- *Wolbachia*-based mosquito programmes are the largest deployed genetic intervention in vector control: a cluster-randomised trial in Yogyakarta reported a 77% reduction in symptomatic dengue in treated areas (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 2021), and the World Mosquito Program operates the releases under national regulatory approval rather than under gene-technology law.
- Genetic rescue has a documented instance at national scale: eight female Texas pumas were translocated into the Florida panther population in 1995, after which inbreeding-associated defects declined and the population increased, and the intervention is now the reference case in recovery planning.
- Small-population genetics supplies the numbers that conservation instruments are written against — effective population size, inbreeding coefficient, the 50/500 rule of thumb for short-term and long-term retention of variation — and these are the quantities a studbook actually manages.
- Hybridisation and introgression are managed as legal categories as well as biological ones: the introgression of non-native barred tiger salamander genes into the California tiger salamander in the Salinas Valley created lineages whose status under the Endangered Species Act had to be determined administratively `[?]`.
- Environmental DNA has become a standard census instrument in California, used in CDFW and academic monitoring for invasive detection and for tracking reintroductions, and it changes what a survey is: a water sample rather than an animal.

## 9. Conservation, banking and de-extinction

- The Svalbard Global Seed Vault holds over 1.4 million seed samples from roughly 70 deposit events and dozens of national genebanks, stored below freezing inside a mountain; it has been drawn on in earnest once, when ICARDA withdrew and later re-deposited its collection after Aleppo became unusable.
- The Frozen Zoo at San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance has banked live cell cultures since the 1970s, and is the source material for every subsequent cloning programme in US endangered-species work.
- The entire living black-footed ferret population descends from seven individuals; tissue banked in 1988 from one female holds roughly three times the genetic diversity of the current population, that animal has been cloned three times, and in 2024 one of the clones bred with a wild-type male and produced two healthy kits — the first offspring of a cloned US endangered species (USFWS, November 2024).
- Revive & Restore, headquartered in Sausalito, runs the USFWS biobanking partnership and the ferret and Przewalski's horse cloning work, so the genetic reserve for a set of American endangered species is administered from a small office on the north shore of San Francisco Bay.
- The AZA Species Survival Plan and studbook system allocates breeding by mean kinship, which means an individual animal's reproductive future is determined by a pedigree calculation performed centrally and issued as a recommendation.
- The USDA-ARS National Laboratory for Genetic Resources Preservation at Fort Collins and the National Clonal Germplasm Repository at Davis hold the US crop and clonal collections; California Plant Rescue and the UC Botanical Garden at Berkeley hold the state's rare-plant banking, and the Crop Trust and NordGen administer Svalbard.
- The International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture operates a multilateral system with a Standard Material Transfer Agreement, so a seed leaving a genebank moves under a contract that follows it and its descendants.
- The Endangered Species Act supplies the operative instruments: listing and critical habitat designation, section 7 interagency consultation, section 9 take prohibitions, section 10 incidental take permits and habitat conservation plans, section 10(j) experimental population designations, and 4(d) rules that tailor protections for threatened species.
- The monarch butterfly was proposed for listing as threatened with a section 4(d) rule and proposed critical habitat on 12 December 2024; the western overwintering count fell from millions in the 1980s to 1,901 in 2020, with 9,119 in 2024 and 12,260 in 2025 — three of the lowest counts in the programme's 28-year history falling in the last six years — and the count itself is performed by volunteers over a fixed late-November window.
- Long-run insect monitoring in German protected areas found a decline of more than 75% in flying insect biomass over 27 years (*PLOS One*, 2017), with meta-analyses putting terrestrial insect abundance decline near 9% per decade and freshwater insects increasing, so the trend is real, contested in magnitude, and measured almost entirely by standardised traps and volunteer effort.
- De-extinction as currently practised produces edited relatives rather than restored species: a 2025 announcement of dire wolves rests on a small number of edits to grey wolf genomes, and whether that constitutes de-extinction is contested in the scientific literature; mammoth and thylacine programmes are at earlier stages, and no governance framework specific to released proxy species yet exists.
- Rinderpest is the second disease ever eradicated, declared in 2011, and virus-containing material is now sequestered under an FAO–WOAH approved-holding-facility regime with periodic voluntary destruction rounds and an inventory (CDC *Emerging Infectious Diseases*, 2022) — an engine deliberately switched off and then counted.
- Variola virus is held under a parallel and stricter regime at two WHO-authorised repositories, subject to biennial WHO inspection and to a World Health Assembly decision on destruction that has been deferred repeatedly since 1993.
- Negligible senescence is documented rather than aspirational in a small number of lineages: naked mole rat mortality hazard does not rise with age in the way the Gompertz model predicts, a Greenland shark has been aged at roughly 392 ± 120 years by eye-lens radiocarbon, and *Turritopsis dohrnii* can reverse its life cycle to the polyp stage; the Buck Institute for Research on Aging in Novato is the regional centre for the comparative work.
- Long dormancy is likewise documented: a nematode revived from Siberian permafrost was dated to about 46,000 years (*PLOS Genetics*, 2023), giant viruses have been revived from 30,000-year permafrost, and the Beal seed viability experiment begun in 1879 continues on a schedule of buried bottles excavated at lengthening intervals.
- The popular account of mycorrhizal common networks — that mature trees preferentially transfer resources to kin through fungal connections — was found by a 2023 review to rest on positive citation bias and overinterpretation of a thin evidential base (*Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2023), and the underlying fact that mycorrhizal fungi connect root systems is not in dispute.
- IUCN's Red List categories and criteria supply the quantitative thresholds that most of these instruments key to, and the newer Green Status of Species scores recovery rather than extinction risk, so a species can be simultaneously endangered and improving on two official scales.

## 10. Invasion and range shift

- San Francisco Bay is routinely described as the most invaded estuary in the world, with a non-indigenous fauna assembled from Atlantic oyster shipments beginning in 1869, ballast water, and hull fouling, and with the soft-shell clam arriving in 1874 as the best-dated single introduction.
- The South Bay Salt Pond Restoration Project is the largest tidal-wetland restoration on the West Coast: 15,100 acres acquired in 2003 for $100 million, a 50-year adaptive-management programme, and 3,770 of a targeted 7,500 acres restored to date.
- New Almaden, one of the largest mercury mines in the northern hemisphere, was worked from 1845 and supplied the quicksilver for the Gold Rush; roughly 90% of the mercury load in the Guadalupe watershed is mining-derived, and opening ponds to the tide can erode sloughs and remobilise that legacy inventory into a system that methylates it.
- The project's answer is engineered and monitored: Pond A8's structure has eight movable gates closed from December through May, and tern-egg mercury fell 60% between 2011 and 2013 with no attributable increase in slough fish.
- The administering bodies are USFWS (Don Edwards San Francisco Bay National Wildlife Refuge), CDFW, the State Coastal Conservancy, the Santa Clara Valley Water District, BCDC and the San Francisco Bay Regional Water Quality Control Board through its mercury TMDL; the San Francisco Bay Restoration Authority is funded by Measure AA, a parcel tax the region voted for itself.
- The San Francisco Estuary Invasive Spartina Project reduced invasive hybrid cordgrass from 805 acres to under 38 acres across a 70,000-acre project area — about 95% — since treatment began in 2005, replanting more than 450,000 native plants and restoring endangered Ridgway's rail habitat, and it works by identifying at scale which individual plants are the wrong lineage.
- CDFA's Pest Exclusion Branch operates the border protection stations that inspect nearly 20 million vehicles a year entering California — a real, staffed, physical border around a state, run for plant pests.
- The golden mussel *Limnoperna fortunei* was detected at the Port of Stockton in October–November 2024, the first known occurrence in North America, with subsequent detections at O'Neill Forebay; the response has been mandatory watercraft inspection and multi-day quarantine at reservoirs across the state, and the presumed arrival route is ballast water.
- Quagga and zebra mussels have been under a statewide watercraft inspection regime since quagga mussels were found in the Colorado River system in 2007, and reservoir operators impose their own decontamination and quarantine requirements under CDFW authority.
- The IMO Ballast Water Management Convention entered into force on 8 September 2017 and requires ships to meet the D-2 discharge standard of fewer than 10 viable organisms of 50 µm or greater per cubic metre; California adds its own regime through the Marine Invasive Species Act, administered by the State Lands Commission, which has regulated vessel biofouling as well as ballast water since 2017.
- Other established Bay and coastal introductions include the Chinese mitten crab, which reached nuisance abundance in the Bay in the 1990s, the New Zealand mudsnail in inland waters, and the European green crab, whose attempted eradication at Seadrift Lagoon produced a documented population rebound and is the standard cautionary case for partial removal.
- Nutria were rediscovered in the San Joaquin Valley in 2017 after presumed extirpation, and CDFW runs a detection-and-removal programme whose instruments are trapping records, detection dogs and a spatial database rather than a regulation.
- The Lacey Act's injurious wildlife listing is the federal import instrument, Executive Order 13112 established the National Invasive Species Council, and CDFA's A/B/C noxious weed ratings determine whether a given plant triggers mandatory state action, county discretion, or none.
- Terrestrial species have shifted their ranges poleward at a median of about 16.9 km per decade and upslope at about 11 m per decade (*Science*, 2011), while marine species have shifted at a median of about 72 km per decade — an order of magnitude faster, because there is no topography in the way.
- Range shifts in the California Current are documented as species-composition changes rather than as gradual movement, with warm-water species including pyrosomes appearing in large numbers during the 2013–2016 marine heatwave and persisting afterwards.
- Assisted migration — deliberately moving a species beyond its historic range to track climate — has no settled regulatory category in the United States, and the section 10(j) experimental population mechanism is the nearest existing instrument, which is why the debate is conducted in terms of permits rather than in terms of ecology.
