# FOG BELT — REFERENCE REGISTER · ANNEX — THE TERMS

*Evidence for `playbook.md` §0. The definitions that section stands on, with the citations it deliberately does not carry, and the disagreements stated rather than resolved. §0 is written to be read before generating; this is written to be checked.*

*Compiled from published definitions, coinages and critical writing. Where a term has no settled definition the annex says so — that is a finding, not a gap.*

---

### 1. GRIMDARK

**Origin.** From the setting-c tagline, *"In the grim darkness of the far future, there is only war,"* first printed in *Rogue Trader* (1987). The *Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction* marks that derivation "allegedly," and its earliest attestations, around 2008, are fandom usage about anime and superhero reboots — "eviler and grimdark" — not fantasy. The fantasy-genre sense is attested only from about 2014–15. **Grimdark was a fandom sneer before it was a genre label**, and Wiktionary still files the adjective as fandom slang.

**The working definition.** *Grimdark Magazine*'s own, and the most load-bearing statement in the field: *"a bleak story with a glimmer of hope told in a hostile setting featuring morally gray characters."* They add explicitly that grimdark does not imply a level of violence, most certainly does not imply gratuitous or sexual violence, and does not promote nihilism — its characters show *"defiance in the absence of hope."*

**The critical definitions.** Adam Roberts: nobody is honourable and might is right, an anti-Tolkien move. Jared Shurin's three components, the third of which is **protagonist agency** — characters choosing between good and evil. Genevieve Valentine notes the term is contested and often functions as a dismissive label for fantasy dismantling tropes.

**Contested, and by its own practitioners.** Mark Lawrence refuses the term as meaningless: it variously means "this thing I don't approve of," "how close you live to Joe Abercrombie," or "how similar a book's atmosphere is to that of *Game of Thrones*" — and critics "describe the terrible properties of grimdark and then fail to name any book that has those properties." Treat grimdark as a contested label with no settled definition.

**The nihilism charge.** Originates with Leo Grin, *The Bankrupt Nihilism of Our Fallen Fantasists* (2011). The sharpest literary version is Liz Bourke's: grimdark's defining feature is *"a retreat into the valorisation of darkness for darkness's sake,"* producing a nihilism that **absolves characters and readers alike from moral responsibility**. Damien Walter adds the commercial charge — bigger swords, more fighting, bloodier blood, an imperative aimed at adolescent male readers. Joe Abercrombie's rebuttal, *The Value of Grit*, concedes that "grimdark seems by definition to be pejorative" and defends grit as inclusion rather than excess — *"Grit is an inclusion. Not grit is an absence"* — while admitting the dividing line between the worthwhile and the gratuitous "is so fuzzy as to be a blur."

**The counter-terms.** *Hopepunk*, coined by Alexandra Rowland on Tumblr in summer 2017; the glib original was "hopepunk is the opposite of grimdark," refined to kindness and softness not equalling weakness, being kind as a political act. Criticised as vague, as marketing, and as resting on a strawman grimdark. *Noblebright* is older, gaming-derived, with no identified coiner. Both terms frame the axis as a claim about **individual efficacy**: noblebright says one person's actions can make a difference, grimdark says they cannot. Charlie Jane Anders added *sweetweird* in 2022.

**Grey against inverted.** No canonical critical pair exists here; this is the field's vaguest area. What the sources support is a three-way split. *Morally grey*: agents are mixed, the moral order is intact. *Morally inverted or nihilistic*: the moral order is absent or reversed, virtue is punished. And a third state neither term covers — **the moral order is intact, functioning and correct, and the harm passes through it**. That third state has no accepted name in genre criticism, and it is where this project sits.

### 2. DARK FANTASY

Explicitly unstable: "a strict definition for dark fantasy is difficult to pin down." Three incompatible uses coexist. **Charles L. Grant's** — a type of horror story in which humanity is threatened by forces beyond human understanding, adopted partly as a euphemism to escape the visceral associations of "horror." **Karl Edward Wagner's** — sword-and-sorcery with anti-heroic, morally ambiguous protagonists. **Brian Stableford's** — horror elements imported into a *secondary world*, which is where he draws the line against horror proper.

The useful finding: the boundary with horror is drawn by **setting and viewpoint, not by intensity**. Dark fantasy often takes the supernatural being's side, reversing horror's victim perspective.

### 3. COSMIC HORROR, WEIRD, EERIE

**Lovecraft's own definition**, from *Supernatural Horror in Literature*, sets two requirements and both matter: *"A certain atmosphere of breathless and unexplainable dread of outer, unknown forces must be present; and there must be a hint … of that most terrible conception of the human brain — a malign and particular suspension or defeat of those fixed laws of Nature which are our only safeguard against the assaults of chaos."* He explicitly excludes "the literature of mere physical fear and the mundanely gruesome." The test he gives is affective: a profound sense of dread, and of contact with unknown spheres and powers.

**Cosmicism** is the philosophical frame, and its distinction from nihilism is real and worth holding: nihilism denies meaning, while cosmicism asserts human **powerlessness and insignificance** and allows the cosmic entities purposes of their own — merely ones inaccessible to the human characters. S. T. Joshi is the standard authority; Houellebecq's *Against the World, Against Life* is the standard polemic. Caveat: no source shows Lovecraft himself using the word "cosmicism."

**Mark Fisher, *The Weird and the Eerie* (2016)** — the single most useful thing in this annex. The **weird** is "a presence — the presence of *that which does not belong*." The **eerie** is "constituted by a *failure of absence* or by a *failure of presence*. There is something where there should be nothing, or there is nothing where there should be something." The eerie is fundamentally a question about **agency**, and Fisher's exampley eerie entity is capital: *"Capital is at every level an eerie entity: conjured out of nothing, capital nevertheless exerts more influence than any allegedly substantial entity."* He positions both beyond Freud's unheimlich as ontological rather than psychological.

**New weird**: Jeff VanderMeer's working definition — urban secondary-world fiction subverting romanticised place, visceral and in-the-moment, acutely aware of the modern world, requiring "surrender to the weird" located *in* the contemporary world rather than a remote elsewhere.

### 4. TERROR, HORROR, REVULSION

**Radcliffe**, "On the Supernatural in Poetry" (1826): the difference lies "in uncertainty and obscurity, that accompany the first." Terror expands the soul; horror "freezes and nearly annihilates" the faculties. **Devendra Varma** (1966) condensed it to **"awful apprehension" versus "sickening realization."** Stephen King's three-tier version — terror, horror, revulsion — is a pragmatic expansion.

Flag for this project: **Radcliffe's terror requires obscurity**, which is precisely what a fully documented institutional register does not have. A file that states everything plainly has forfeited terror by definition and is working in horror and revulsion only. That is a real constraint, not a preference.

**Noël Carroll's art-horror** (1990) has a two-part requirement: the monster must be **both threatening and impure**, impurity generated by category-jamming, contagion or disgust. Monsters are "un-natural relative to a culture's conceptual scheme of nature. They do not fit the scheme, they violate it." Contested and known to be over-narrow — Carroll excludes *Psycho* from horror for lacking a monster.

### 5. THE ABJECT AND THE UNCANNY

**Kristeva, *Powers of Horror* (1980).** The abject "does not respect borders, positions, rules. The in-between, the ambiguous, the composite," and crucially: *"It is thus not the lack of cleanliness or health that causes abjection but what disturbs identity, system, and order."* The corpse is the limit case. Barbara Creed's *The Monstrous-Feminine* is the standard application to film. The load-bearing point for us: **disgust is a category effect, not an injury effect**, which is why §0 says the disgust is a category failure and blood is not the mechanism.

**The uncanny.** Jentsch (1906) grounds it in **intellectual uncertainty**, paradigmatically about whether a thing is animate — the automaton problem. Freud (1919) rejects that account as insufficient and substitutes the return of the repressed and the homely-made-strange. The disagreement is live, and the Jentsch version is the more useful one for anything that grades, scores or measures interiority.

### 6. BODY HORROR AND ITS NEIGHBOURS

**Body horror** was coined by Philip Brophy in "Horrality: The Textuality of the Contemporary Horror Film" (1983). The critical distinction from splatter is explicit and is *not* about intensity: body horror's violations are marked by a **loss of conscious control over the body** through mutation, disease, or uncontrolled transformation, rather than by immediate violence. Slasher, splatter and monster horror share the mutilation trope but differ in message and intent.

**Splatterpunk** was coined by David J. Schow at the 1986 World Fantasy Convention — hyperintensive horror, the "there are no limits" variety, defined by the fact that it does not dare look away. Its opposite pole is **Charles L. Grant's quiet horror**, which works by suggestion and reader-supplied detail.

### 7. BUREAUCRATIC AND INSTITUTIONAL HORROR — NO SETTLED TERM

This is the honest finding of the whole annex. **There is no accepted genre name for what this project does.**

*Kafkaesque* is the only widely used word and is acknowledged to be grossly overused and frequently misused. The most precise definition on record is Frederick R. Karl's (1991): "What's Kafkaesque is when you enter a surreal world in which all your control patterns, all your plans, the whole way in which you have configured your own behavior, begins to fall to pieces" — and, importantly, *you keep fighting it*. Kafka's core features are bureaucratic opacity, inaccessible authority, guilt without a disclosed charge, and futile struggle: **the system is arbitrary and unjustifiable.** That is the deviation. Ours are legible, audited and correct.

*Arendt's "banality of evil"* names the adjacent thing: atrocity executed by ordinary people not thinking. *Fisher's Capitalist Realism* (2009) supplies the audit-culture version. The only attempted genre coinage found is **"the Institutional Gothic"** (MIT Press Reader) — uncanny spaces, malevolent forces and overwhelming discomfort related to spatiality and power in office environments, exemplified by the Backrooms, *The Stanley Parable* and *Severance*. Presented by its author as a new coinage, not an established term.

### 8. BLEAK, DARK, NIHILISTIC, PESSIMISTIC, TRAGIC

**Bleak** and **dark** are not critical terms. Neither has a definition in the literature; *Grimdark Magazine* uses "bleak" as the genre's baseline atmosphere — weather rather than structure. §0's assertion that bleak is a mood and wears off is therefore sound, but it is a house position rather than a citation.

**Nihilistic** does have a definition and is the specific charge levelled at the genre: darkness valorised for its own sake, absolving characters and readers of moral responsibility.

**Pessimistic** is distinct and better defined. Eugene Thacker's *cosmic pessimism* — "the night-side of thought … a poetry written in the graveyard of philosophy," a pessimism of the world-without-us — retains meaning while denying purchase, which nihilism does not. This is closer to the register than nihilism is.

**Tragic** is the most rigorously defined and the most damaging to test against.

### 9. THE ARISTOTLE CONSTRAINT

The finding that most constrains this project, and the reason it is in the annex rather than left out.

*Poetics* XIII rules out the exact configuration the register is built on: *"the change of fortune presented must not be the spectacle of a virtuous man brought from prosperity to adversity: for this moves neither pity nor fear; **it merely shocks us.**"* The Greek is **μιαρόν**, *miaron* — polluted, repugnant. The tragic protagonist must fall "not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty," and the best tragedies turn on *anagnorisis*, recognition.

A faultless victim destroyed by a faultless machine, with no recognition available, is by the oldest standard in the field **not tragedy but revulsion**. This is checkable, it is the single strongest objection to the register, and §0 holds it rather than routing around it: the answer is not to soften the harm or to introduce a flaw, but to implicate the reader — to put them inside the arrangement, using it, approving of it, having signed off on it. A reader who can stand outside and be appalled has been handed *miaron*. A reader who cannot get out has been handed dread.

### 10. WHERE THIS PROJECT SITS

**Not grimdark.** By the genre's own working definition — bleak world, morally grey characters, a glimmer reached for in defiance — the register fails two of three criteria and inverts the third. Its people are not grey; they are decent, competent, correctly motivated and often right. There is no defiance, because the register removes the thing defiance acts against: Shurin's protagonist agency, choosing between good and evil, is exactly what the corpus deletes. The noblebright/grimdark axis is a dispute about whether one person's action can make a difference. This project is not on that axis.

**Cosmicism relocated into administration.** The engine is Lovecraft's — indifference rather than malevolence, human scale as an error term, the epistemic wound where what is taken is the faculty that would have registered the taking — with the supernatural stripped out. It deviates from Lovecraft on the one thing he required: **nothing is suspended**. No law of nature is defeated. The turbidity really was a safety. The pig organs really are clean. The grading rubric really works. That is the house signature, and it is also the deviation from Kafka, whose systems are arbitrary and unjustifiable where these are legible, audited and correct.

**The closest single term is Fisher's eerie** — a failure of presence, a question about agency whose true answer is nobody, the shape capital already occupies. The kindness-as-a-variable instance is a precise inversion of hopepunk's central claim.

**The standing risk is Aristotelian**, and §0's ceiling clause exists to answer it.
