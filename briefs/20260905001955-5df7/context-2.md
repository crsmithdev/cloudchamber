*Job: Tests the arithmetic as engine: Anselm on day 84, one day's walk short of anything, deciding whether to spend his last march testing the wall's range or feeding it a correction.*

On the eighty-fourth day the road was dry and he made twenty miles by None, and the wall was still there.

It had finished with him on the first day, all thirty thousand words, and it had been repeating since. His mother at the stones, the year he entered. Brother Odo, who had gone out in Hugh's last Lent and stood at the east face and pressed in everything he knew of the men still inside, including a thin novice who was afraid of the dark stair. Eleven men, each leaving him to the wall like a coat. And under them, newer, the sound of his own walking, coming back to him as sentences.

*Twelve miles in snow. Twenty on the dry. Twelve hundred and sixty miles from the gate. The one before you stopped at one hundred and forty.*

Tomorrow was Ascension. He had walked eighty-four days against that number, eaten against it, chosen the ford over the bridge against it, and it had not been wrong yet about anything.

He sat on the milestone and considered the last day.

He could walk out. One more twenty miles and see whether the voice thinned. No sent-out man had found the edge of it, and he was further than any of the four hundred and ten. But he saw the flaw as the wall would see it: if the voice stopped tomorrow he would not know whether he had left its hearing or it had left his, and there would be no one to tell the difference to. The test and the death fell on the same day. The wall had arranged that without meaning to. Arithmetic does not mean.

Or he could turn.

Not toward the house. Toward the ground. Whatever was pressed in was retained, and what was retained was delivered, and it was delivered to one person only: the next man out, standing in the snow with his hand on the ridges, being told he had until some feast.

Anselm knew what neither archive knew. The graves at the parish were a list. The whitewash was a seal. The number was right. The abbot read the letters. Odo had known the first two and had not lived to walk the third.

He stood. He walked a slow mile back the way he had come, and then forward, and then back, the way a man paces when he is composing, and he let the wall have it: *The count is correct. The house is an ear. It hears only us. Whoever is reading this, you are the four hundred and twelfth; do not walk to the coast. Walk to the sexton.*

It took him until dark to say it with his feet. Then he lay down beside the road on day eighty-four, and did not test anything.
