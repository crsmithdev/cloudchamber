*Job: Tests the custody chain's one-way gate: the abbot reading a forwarded exile's letter, recognizing the wall's function, and ordering the Advent whitewash anyway, the seal being the office's only answer.*

The visitation came in the third week of Advent, as it always did, in the bishop's clerk's hand: the accounts approved, the enclosure regretted, and a folded sheet at the back with a note that the parish priest at the coast had forwarded it as the ravings of a mute vagrant, deceased, and the bishop thought the abbot should know his sons were dying in ditches with Latin in their pockets.

The abbot knew that. He had the list.

The Latin was good. The hand was Brother Wilfrid's, who had gone out at the last sending and was to have lived until Pentecost, and had. It was addressed to no one. *The wall hears,* it said. *Not with an ear; it is the ear. It keeps what is pressed into it and gives it back to the hand as speech. It kept my walking. It told me the day. Tell them to go round to the east face and lay their hands on it and they will hear everything the world has said of them, and they will hear me. Do not let them plaster it. I think the plaster is why we do not know.*

There was more, about a woman who had fed him, and the sea. The abbot read it twice and then sat with it in his lap while the light went.

He had entered by the west gate at nineteen, like all of them, told nothing. He had never stood at the east face. No one inside had. He counted, because the Chapter book let him count: four hundred and ten coats of lime since the founding, a fifth of a millimetre each, and the founding rule in its own words, *in the figure of the ear, that the world may speak and we be not troubled.* Eight centimetres. Thicker than a hand.

He went down to the church and put his palm flat against the inside of the east wall, where Wilfrid's name and the others were pressed into the far side of the stone. It was cold and white and said nothing. It had never said anything. That was what the plaster was for.

He could order it stripped. He was the one man in the house who could, and the one man who would never be sent to stand outside and check.

He went back up and wrote nothing in the Chapter book. He put Wilfrid's letter in the chest with the others, the ones no abbot had handed on because there was no one to hand them to. Then he sent for the cellarer and told him the lime was to be slaked by Saturday, the whole east face, two coats, as the rubric said, so the brothers might have the wall clean for Christmas and not be troubled.
