The camera records Tape 12. Walt teaches the five steps on the east line and grafts Bev Sorensen to a cedar post while fourteen-year-old Lyle keeps her in the middle third of the frame.

My father holds the slate up himself: BRULE CO. AG EXTENSION. TAPE 12. 4/11/87. HASKETT PLACE, EAST LINE. Then he steps back into frame and waits for my nod.

"Step one," he tells the lens. "Choose your post while the ground's still soft. Cedar or locust. Set it before the frost goes out." He puts a hand on the corner post, then on the one west of it. "These went in March. You can see the soil's still working."

The JVC brace digs into my shoulder. I'm fourteen, and all I care about is keeping the picture from shaking.

"Step two. Choose your scion."

Bev Sorensen steps into the middle third in her church coat. She's been his assistant since before I was old enough to drive the truck. She smiles at the lens because he tells her to. Then she keeps smiling, because he never tells her to stop.

"Step three. The cut."

He takes off her coat and folds it over the fence wire. He turns her wrists up the way he'd hold a whip of Haralson, and he runs the knife along each one, shallow and clean. Don't follow the hands, Lyle. I don't. His hands come in from the edges of the frame and go back out, and Bev stays centered, looking at me.

What runs out of her isn't red for long.

"Step four. The binding." He brings her wrists back to the cedar where he's already notched it, and I understand the notches were cut to fit her. Baling twine, three turns and a half hitch. He works the grafting wax in with his thumb until the twine doesn't show. Then he kneels, pulls the soil up over her shoes, and tamps it down with the flat of his hand. He always says a sloppy job invites rot.

"Walt," Bev says. She's still smiling. "It's cold down there."

"It will be for a while." He stands and brushes off his knees. "Step five is the waiting. The union takes by the second spring. If it doesn't take, you make a fresh cut. Nobody wants that, not the post and not the scion." He looks straight down the lens, at the farm bureau men, at the county, at me.

The cedar smells like Christmas. The welt on my shoulder will last a week.
