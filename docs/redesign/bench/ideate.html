A surveying error: the subdivision was platted over a child's grave, and the county's software now lists the boy as a parcel owner. His mother's affair partner, a title clerk, tries to quietly clear the deed and instead schedules something—the forms require a "site visit with the minor stakeholder." The visit occurs. Afterward only she remains, walking the perimeter of Lot 14's skeleton frame, and the earth beneath her heels re-grades itself each night, contour lines migrating on the plat map, funneling her toward a threshold that was never framed for a door.

The plat map is printed at 1:100 and Yolanda has it laminated now, because of the dew.

Lot 14's contour lines have moved again. She knows the old shape by heart—two lazy parentheses cupping the northeast corner, a half-foot rise where the footing drain was supposed to daylight. This morning the parentheses have closed. They make an O, and the O is not centered on the survey monument. It is centered on her.

She walks the perimeter because walking the perimeter is what a title clerk does. Boundary by inspection. Adverse possession runs on open, notorious, continuous use, and she has been continuous for eleven days.

Told entirely in the drywall. A framing crew's unfinished spec home keeps its own records: shims, pencil marks, a stud scrawled with two names and a date. A woman comes weekly to walk the muddy perimeter—her boy died the summer the slab was poured, and her husband's mistress brought a nephew here to play, once, and only the mistress drove home. The house knows the mother is circling. It has been withholding its stairs for years, waiting. Each rain, the grade rises an inch. Each rain, the lot gets smaller and the doorway gets nearer.
40.04
The dead boy's mother and her lover keep the play date on the calendar—six years running, a standing appointment with an empty backyard. This year the other child arrives: a neighbor's toddler, delivered by a mother who cannot say why she came. The two women watch from the unfinished house next door, the one that never got siding, while the toddler plays with someone. Afterward the survivor cannot leave the property. Every direction she walks, the graded dirt tilts, subtly, patiently, until she is facing the doorway again. The house is not finished because it is still deciding on her dimensions.

Both children come back, in a sense—one wearing the other's laugh.
